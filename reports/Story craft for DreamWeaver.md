# Design, not horsepower, lifts DreamWeaver's stories

Better DreamWeaver stories need changes to prompts and design first, a stronger model only for one or two calls per story, and more reasoning effort last. The failures measured in LLM fiction (arcs that are too happy, too few setbacks, turning points that come too early, and stories that resemble each other) are the failures an unplanned, turn-by-turn narrator is most exposed to, and the best-supported fixes are structural: a planned arc, scheduled setbacks, state that looks forward, and variety injected by code. Those fixes cost fractions of a cent per story. The craft literature agrees on a small core: events linked by cause, a protagonist who wants something and pays for trying, promises that are kept, and an ending built from what the story already holds. Almost none of that core has been tested experimentally, and the famous templates (Hero's Journey, Save the Cat, Freytag's pyramid) are the contested part. Interactive-fiction and tabletop practice supply the turn-level rules: show the consequence of each choice at once, signal the stakes inside the options, remember what the reader did, and advance a threat when the story stalls. The evidence on learning is firmer and more sobering: stories help when the concept drives the plot and hurt when it is bolted on, so lesson packs must specify situations and tempting wrong choices, not only facts. Much of the source material was reached through summaries, abstracts or secondary write-ups, and nothing in it tests a small 2026 model in a one-call-per-turn children's storybook, so every recommendation here is a hypothesis to check with blind comparisons and the app's own telemetry.

## Good stories share a small core, and the famous templates are the contested part

### Structure models describe families of stories, not a universal law

Each popular structure model began as a description of one kind of story and was later promoted to a template. Aristotle's requirement is the durable one: each event should be the "necessary or probable" result of what came before, and a reversal should surprise yet follow from the plot ([SparkNotes on the Poetics](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [CriticaLink](https://english.hawaii.edu/criticalink/aristotle/gloss/gloss11.html)). Freytag's pyramid analysed five-act tragedy and puts the climax in the middle, which does not match most modern fiction ([TECFA narrative wiki](https://tecfalabs.unige.ch/mediawiki-narrative/index.php/Five-act_model)). **The Hero's Journey is contested as a universal pattern**: classicists writing in the LA Review of Books argue Campbell cherry-picked myths to fit a preset frame and centred a single masculine, individualist protagonist ([LARB](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-man-behind-the-myth-should-we-question-the-heros-journey)). Folklorists' dismissals of Campbell are reported through secondary sources that the notes did not check against the originals ([Wikipedia: Hero's journey](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Monomyth)). Save the Cat's fifteen page-locked beats were blamed by Slate for interchangeable blockbusters, though Snyder called the sheet "a structure, not a formula" ([Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/culturebox/2013/07/hollywood_and_blake_snyder_s_screenwriting_book_save_the_cat.html)).

The compact models fit DreamWeaver better. Dan Harmon's Story Circle (You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change) was written to help people tell a complete story in about five minutes ([Channel 101 wiki](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit)). The Pixar "story spine" runs "Once upon a time... Every day... One day... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally..." ([NYFA list of Emma Coats's rules](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)). Kishotenketsu (introduction, development, twist, reconciliation) draws its interest from a surprise and how it connects, without needing a driving antagonist; the claim that it has "no conflict" comes from a 2012 blog post and overstates the case ([Wikipedia: Kishotenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); [The Rumpus](https://therumpus.net/2013/03/can-you-tell-a-story-without-conflict)). Le Guin's "carrier bag" essay and Matthew Salesses's *Craft in the Real World* make the wider point that conflict-centred, single-hero plotting is one tradition's expectation, not a neutral rule ([Le Guin](https://wiki.xxiivv.com/docs/carrier_bag.html); [Sydney Review of Books](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/review/making-craft-conscious)).

The only model with quantitative backing is the emotional arc. Reagan and colleagues ran sentiment analysis over about 1,300 English-language Project Gutenberg novels and found **six recurring arc shapes** (rise, fall, fall-rise, rise-fall, rise-fall-rise, fall-rise-fall), with some shapes downloaded more than others ([arXiv 1606.07772](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772); [UVM appendix](https://www.uvm.edu/storylab/share/papers/reagan2016b/index.html)). The study is correlational, uses word sentiment as a stand-in for reader emotion, and covers old adult novels. It shows which shapes are common, not that imposing one makes a story better. The notes could not verify which arcs drew the most downloads, so this report makes no claim about that.

| Model | Status | Use in DreamWeaver |
|---|---|---|
| Causal chain (Aristotle, "therefore/but", story spine) | Craft consensus from independent sources; untested | Default rule on every turn |
| Emotional arc shapes | Empirical, with the limits above | A per-story dial: pick a shape, place the low point about two thirds through |
| Story spine / Story Circle | Craft consensus as compact tools | Skeleton for quest and adventure stories of 5-20 scenes |
| Kishotenketsu | Real tradition; "no conflict" framing contested | Skeleton for gentle and bedtime stories |
| Hero's Journey, Save the Cat, Freytag | Contested as general models | One optional template among several; never hard-coded |

### Character, causality and stakes carry more weight than any template

Practitioners agree that readers attach to characters who want something and act, more than to characters who are likeable or successful. Pixar's rules say it plainly: "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes," and passive characters are "poison to the audience" ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)). The Writing Excuses podcast argues that visible failure builds empathy ([Writing Excuses 10.29](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/)). The screenwriting schema of "want versus need" is useful shorthand but contested: John August calls it a false framework that does not help write scenes ([johnaugust.com](https://johnaugust.com/2008/rethinking-motivation)). The related research measures absorption ("transportation") and identification with characters as distinct states that correlate with liking protagonists, though most of that work concerns persuasion and attempts to manipulate transportation have often failed ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6999344)). No study in the notes tests flaws, arc types or want/need against reader enjoyment.

**The most transferable rule is causal linkage.** Trey Parker and Matt Stone told an NYU class that if "and then" belongs between two beats the story has a problem, and that beats should join with "therefore" or "but" (the notes hold press paraphrase, not a transcript) ([Cracked](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html); [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/south-park-creators-visit-nyu-class-lessons-storytelling-30806)). George Saunders adds escalation: a passage earns its place by changing the terms of the story, and a recurring element should return varied, never repeated ([The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)). Genre writers resolve attempts as "yes, but" (progress with a complication) or "no, and" (failure that worsens things), and warn against applying it as a checklist ([Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/)). Dwight Swain's "scene and sequel" alternates a unit of goal, conflict and disaster with a unit of reaction, dilemma and decision ([Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)). Stakes need stating: "What happens if they don't succeed?" ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)). All of this is practitioner wisdom. The nearest experiments concern spoilers: a 2011 study found readers enjoyed spoiled stories more, and later studies disagree, so uncertainty about the outcome is not the only source of pleasure ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html); [Levine lab non-replication](https://wordpressua.uark.edu/whlevine/?p=122)). A separate line of research ties the reader's feeling to the order of information: withholding an outcome produces suspense, showing an outcome before its cause produces curiosity, and an unsignalled reveal produces surprise ([Brewer and Lichtenstein summary](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)).

Scene and sequel maps almost exactly onto a DreamWeaver turn, and this is the most valuable mapping in the notes. The narration is the outcome of the last choice plus a beat of reaction, the three choices are the dilemma, and the reader's pick is the decision that sets the next goal. The narrator prompt should therefore say what each scene's sentences must do: open with a consequence of the reader's choice or a complication to it and never with an unrelated event, resolve the attempt as "yes, but" or "no, and" and never as plain success, change one thing (what is known, what is at risk, or a relationship), and stop on an open question. The story's opening scenes should state the protagonist's want and what is lost if they fail, scaled to the reader. For a five-year-old, a kite that will blow away before the picnic is a full stake.

### Setups are promises, and endings pay them

Chekhov's gun is among the least disputed craft ideas: do not put a loaded rifle on stage if nobody will fire it. Its famous wording comes from reminiscences, so it should not be quoted as exact ([Wikipedia: Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun)). Applied rigidly it strips texture and makes every detail a signal ([Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/dont-shoot-why-i-dont-believe-in-chekhovs-gun)). Brandon Sanderson's "promises, progress, payoff" is the formulation closest to an instruction: the opening promises a tone, genre and kind of conflict, the middle shows visible progress, and the ending pays off (the notes rely on show notes, not a transcript) ([Odyssey Workshop podcast](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=22073)). Pixar's rule 19 covers the payoff side: coincidences that get characters into trouble are fine, and coincidences that get them out "are cheating" ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)). The contested item is J.J. Abrams's "mystery box", whose critics argue that the longer an answer is withheld, the bigger the payoff must be; those critiques are opinion pieces ([TED](https://www.ted.com/talks/j_j_abrams_the_mystery_box?language=en); [Birth.Movies.Death](https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2016/01/12/we-created-the-mystery-box.html)).

An improvising narrator makes the mystery problem worse, because nobody has decided the answer. The rule for DreamWeaver is that the narrator raises a question only when it records the answer at the same moment, in a hidden field, and resolves it within a bounded number of scenes. A five-scene story can carry one setup and payoff. A long story can carry three to five threads, which matches the bible's existing cap. The final page should answer the story's central question, use at least one earlier setup, show what changed for the protagonist, echo an image or phrase from the first scene, and name one specific choice the reader made. That last item is the cheapest way to make an interactive ending feel earned, and it is this report's inference from the craft sources, not a tested finding.

### Prose and theme: specific, economical, and unannounced

"Show, don't tell" is over-applied. The moon-and-broken-glass line attributed to Chekhov is a paraphrase of a letter ([Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2013/07/30/moon-glint/)), and critics treat the rule as a stylistic preference. The usable core is narrower: one concrete, specific detail at moments that matter, and free summary elsewhere. Economy has broad support among practitioners: "Simplify. Focus. Combine characters. Hop over detours," and "discount the 1st thing that comes to mind" ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)). The second rule matters for a language model, which defaults to the most probable detail. On theme, practitioners hold that meaning comes from what characters choose and what it costs; Saunders's example is that "the king died of grief" means the king loved the queen without anyone saying so ([The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)). No experiment in the notes tests implicit against stated themes for enjoyment. In DreamWeaver the theme lives in the choices: give the narrator a one-line thematic question at story start (safety or kindness, alone or together), make some choices test it, and let the ending reflect the pattern of what the reader chose.

## Choices satisfy when they are informed, remembered and costly

### What makes a choice meaningful

Nearly everything in this field is practitioner wisdom; the notes found no controlled studies of which properties of a choice raise satisfaction. The practitioners agree closely, though. Sid Meier defines an interesting decision by what it is not: a choice the player always answers the same way, or answers at random. He adds that "the worst thing you can do is just move on" after a choice ([Game Developer on Meier's GDC 2012 talk](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)). Choice of Games' rules say every option needs real consequences, the player needs a basis for choosing, no option should be obviously best or worst, and "opt-out" options are dull; even the wrong choice should make the reader say "Wow, that was neat!" ([Choice of Games, 5 Rules](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2010/03/5-rules-for-writing-interesting-choices-in-multiple-choice-games/)). Their follow-up names the worst case, bare "Go right" or "Go left", and the cure: put clues to the risk and reward in the option text or the sentence before it ([Choice of Games, Intentional Choices](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2016/12/how-to-write-intentional-choices/)). Meg Jayanth, writer of *80 Days*, pushes the other way on safety: "My job as a writer was to tempt players into making bad decisions," because near-misses are what players remember ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/80-days-writer-tempt-your-players-into-making-bad-decisions/)).

Whether choices must change the plot is disputed. Telltale's games were criticised because "major decisions often don't have lasting consequences" despite advertising that the story is tailored to the player ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2015/02/03/why-your-choices-dont-matter-in-telltale-games.aspx)), and defended as a series of moral dilemmas whose value is expressive ([Eludamos](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/eludamos/article/view/vol9no1-2)). Jon Ingold of inkle says choices exist to make players invested and take ownership, and that stories should respond in surprising ways ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/should-games-have-meaningful-choices-)). The lesson for DreamWeaver is to never announce that a choice mattered and then forget it. A generative narrator can honour any choice at no production cost; its risk is forgetting.

The rules for DreamWeaver's three suggestions follow. The three should be different kinds of move (bold, careful, clever; or kind, curious, loyal), not three flavours of one. Each names an action and hints at its stake: "Cross the rope bridge before the storm hits" beats "Go across". None is a free lunch, a trap, an opt-out or a blind door. One reveals character, one may be the tempting or strange one and must lead somewhere good for the story, and at least one touches the bible by using a carried item, a known character or an open thread. Because readers can also type an action, the suggestions need not cover every possibility; their job is to model interesting moves. The notes found no well-sourced rule on the ideal number of options, and nothing on choice-writing for young readers, so the "three" is consistent with practice but not validated.

### Agency and arc are reconciled through state

Interactive-fiction designers reconcile reader freedom with a shaped story by letting choices change state instead of forking the plot. Sam Kabo Ashwell's catalogue of structures describes the unmanaged, ever-branching "time cave" as giving the reader "velocity but little grasp", and "branch and bottleneck" (branches rejoin at shared events, with remembered state) as the pattern that lets a reader build a distinctive story ([Ashwell](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)). Choice of Games calls the same idea delayed branching and gives a rule of thumb: "Every chapter should change the stats and test them to make earlier decisions meaningful" ([Choice of Games, By the Numbers](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2011/07/by-the-numbers-how-to-write-a-long-interactive-novel-that-doesnt-suck/)). Emily Short's storylets are content plus prerequisites plus effects on world state, and her examples track a progress value and a menace value ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/)). Failbetter's writer guidelines say two tracked qualities is "the magic minimum" for engaging structure, and warn that a string of changes in one result cheapens each ([Failbetter](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-ii)). Keith Johnstone's improvisation rule gives the same advice from the stage: the improviser is "like a man walking backwards" who shapes a story "by remembering incidents that have been shelved and reincorporating them" ([Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)).

An unmanaged DreamWeaver story is a time cave: anything is possible and nothing accumulates. The story bible is what moves it toward branch and bottleneck, but only if the narrator tests what the bible records. Three rules follow. Every few scenes, something recorded earlier (an item, an ally, a promise, a trait the hero has shown) should decide how a scene turns out. After the midpoint, each scene should reuse something from the bible before it introduces anything new, and the ending should be built from shelved material only. An item that never comes back should not have been recorded, and a cost should be paid in something the bible holds (a lost item, a strained friendship, time against a threat), because an unrecorded cost is forgotten.

### Tabletop game-masters already solved improvising without a plot

The tabletop tradition closest to DreamWeaver's design rejects pre-written plots. Dungeon World's game-master agenda includes "play to find out what happens", and its list of moves reads like a menu for a narrator's turn: "reveal an unwelcome truth", "show signs of an approaching threat", "use up their resources", "offer an opportunity, with or without cost", "tell them the requirements or consequences and ask". Its soft-move rule is a fairness principle: a warning comes first, and the hard consequence lands only if the warning is ignored ([Dungeon World SRD](https://www.dungeonworldsrd.com/gamemastering/)). Its "fronts" record threats with an impulse, a list of grim portents and an impending doom. Apocalypse World and Blades in the Dark track such threats as clocks that tick when trouble grows: early ticks are "coming, but preventable", late ones are "inevitable, but there's still time to brace for impact" ([The Daily Apocalypse](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/85-threat-countdown-clocks); [Blades in the Dark SRD](https://bladesinthedark.com/progress-clocks)). Justin Alexander's advice is to prepare a situation with opposition that wants something, not a plot, and to make any essential discovery reachable three ways ([Don't Prep Plots](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots); [Three Clue Rule](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)). Johnstone's term for refusing a partner's idea is blocking, "a form of aggression" ([*Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)).

These sources endorse DreamWeaver's no-outline design on one condition: the narrator must establish a situation early, meaning a goal, an opposing force with its own aim, and a few resources and places. They also reshape the bible's open threads. A thread recorded as "the missing key" gives the narrator nothing to advance. A thread recorded as a front does: "the tide is rising toward the cave where the key is hidden; next it floods the lower path; if ignored, the key is lost." One main threat with a four-step clock, plus lighter threads, is closer to tabletop practice than five equal threads. When the reader dawdles or stalls, the clock ticks and the next scene shows a sign of it, tied to an existing thread and not newly invented. For typed actions, the narrator accepts and adds a consequence, accepts at a cost, or declines the literal action while offering something in its spirit. A flat refusal is a block. The named ladder of "yes, and / yes, but / no, but" is community vocabulary with no single authoritative source in the notes.

### Research adds a hidden manager with a target tension curve

The research framing matches the practitioners'. Riedl and Bulitko's survey calls the core challenge balancing "a coherent story progression with user agency", and describes a drama manager that looks ahead and picks the continuation that best fits the author's criteria, which is often not the most likely one ([Riedl and Bulitko, AI Magazine 2013](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~riedl/pubs/aimag.pdf)). A language model continuing with the most likely next sentence does the opposite. Façade, the best-known system of this kind, sequenced about 18 beats over 20 minutes, scored candidate beats against a target tension arc, and gave each beat a body that poses "a dramatic question or situation to the player" ([Mateas and Stern 2003](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~isbell/classes/reading/papers/MateasSternGDC03.pdf)). Its pacing rule is usable directly: the target change in tension per beat is the target tension minus the current tension, divided by the beats remaining. These are system descriptions and a survey, not user studies, and the notes found no evaluation showing that tension-curve management raises satisfaction.

For DreamWeaver this argues for two hidden numbers in the bible, progress through the arc and current trouble, and for the application to compute the phase and target in code and state it in the prompt each turn. Façade's 18 beats is the closest designed analogue to a single sitting, which suggests a soft default of roughly 15 to 20 scenes; this is an analogy from one system. The notes found no data on how long casual interactive-story sessions run or on ideal scene counts. The "Bring the story to a close" button should set beats remaining to a small number so the narrator converges over one to three scenes, and Emily Short's caution applies: an ending assembled from whatever is salient lands flat next to one an author placed ([Emily Short, Beyond Branching](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). The current single final page is the tightest version of this, so it needs the most help: the ending call should receive the open threads, the planted secret, the first scene and the reader's most characteristic choices.

## Young readers need fewer words, visible safety and an adult nearby

### Picture-book craft fits the format DreamWeaver already has

Picture-book advice is craft consensus, untested by experiment, and it lines up with DreamWeaver's page format. Mem Fox's alarm bell rings at 500 words for a whole book; she says to stay "out of the illustrator's way", use "said", write "with narrative tension: solve a problem", and not "write to teach" because "children and publishers detest heavy morals". She warns against plotting in rhyme, never ends on "and then they all woke up", and treats rhythm heard aloud as the test of a draft ([Mem Fox, Writing Picture Books](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books); [Do's and Don'ts](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)). Publishers allow more, 500 to 1,000 words ([Penguin UK](https://penguin.co.uk/about/company-articles/how-to-write-a-children-s-picture-book)). Editors treat the page turn as the unit of suspense, and the child protagonist, not an adult, should solve the problem, usually after failed attempts ([Nathan Bransford](https://nathanbransford.com/blog/2018/03/picture-books-work-brief-primer); [Tara Lazar on the rule of three](https://taralazar.com/2020/07/16/break-the-rules-the-rule-of-threes/)).

A DreamWeaver scene is already a picture-book spread, and the choice sits where the page turn sits. A little-readers story of 12 to 16 scenes lands in the recommended word range. The prompt for that level should ask for short sentences in spoken order that can each be said in one breath, familiar words with at most one interesting new word per scene, plain "said", a short refrain that returns every few scenes and varies at the climax, no rhyme, no stated moral, and a child hero who acts while adults do not fix things. A poor choice leads to a funny setback and another try, never a dead end, which also supplies the repeated attempts. Do not generate to a readability score: researchers find formulas measure surface features and are not "a reliable guide for editing a text to reduce its difficulty" ([Anderson and Davison](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/17909/bitstreams/64198/data.pdf)). The notes found no sourced table of sentence lengths by age, so the exact limits are a design judgement.

### What frightens and amuses changes with age

Joanne Cantor's research on television and film, mostly from the 1990s and reached here through summaries, found that younger children are frightened by how things look, while older children weigh behaviour and respond to realistic and abstract dangers. Young children are comforted by physical reassurance, older ones by reasoning ([Encyclopedia.com, Fear and the Media](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media); [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/scare-tactics/1996/02)). Sendak and Bettelheim argue the opposing case, that children should not be shielded from frightening material ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/114044628)). The notes found no equivalent study for read-aloud or illustrated text. Humour develops from visible incongruity to riddles and wordplay around six or seven, and toward irony by about nine ([Paine et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8360189); [Purser et al.](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10087338/3/Van%20Herwegen%20Riddles%20Article%20for%20JECP%20ACCEPTED%20VERSION.pdf)).

The three reading levels should therefore differ in more than vocabulary. For little readers the danger is visual, so creatures stay rounded and comic, peril is mild and resolves within a scene or two, comfort is concrete (a companion, a warm light), and outcomes are mostly "yes, but". Family stories can carry real, resolvable stakes, riddles and light irony, and a low point. Confident readers can have dilemmas with lasting costs, morally mixed antagonists and "no, and" outcomes, within the all-ages ceiling. What stays constant is economy, speakable rhythm, an open beat at the end of each scene, and a protagonist who drives events.

### Pictures should carry what words leave out, and match them for the youngest

Picture-book scholars describe a range of word-image relationships from symmetrical (each repeats the other) through complementary to counterpoint, a typology the notes took from secondary summaries ([Nikolajeva and Scott](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1023%2FA%3A1026426902123.pdf)). Mac Barnett adds a rule on restraint: gruesome material that works in words "becomes too gross" when drawn ([BookPage](https://bookpage.com/interviews/mac-barnett-jon-klassen-interview-the-three-billy-goats-gruff/)). The research side is firmer. A meta-analysis of 43 studies found that story-congruent multimedia raised young children's comprehension (g = 0.39), while interactive features did not (g = -0.14, not significant) and the authors judged they "distract from understanding the story" ([Takacs, Swart and Bus 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)). A later meta-analysis found that adult mediation of print reading beat enhancements in digital books read alone ([Furenes, Kucirkova and Bus 2021](https://oro.open.ac.uk/75652)), and a lab study found parents talk less about the story with tablet books than with print ([Munzer et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6564071)). Dialogic reading, in which an adult prompts the child to talk about the story, has a moderate effect on expressive vocabulary in a meta-analysis reached through a secondary review ([Mol et al. 2008](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2899524/view)).

DreamWeaver's single choice at the scene boundary is not the tap-anywhere hotspot those studies tested, so the negative finding does not transfer cleanly, but it argues for keeping interaction at the boundary and adding nothing decorative. The image prompt should own place, time, weather, appearance, expression and one extra detail to spot. The narration should own names, speech, inner feeling, sound, smell and cause. For little readers the picture should show the exact moment in the text with the hero and the key object clearly visible. Counterpoint belongs to older levels. Molly Bang's composition principles, taken from course summaries, give mood instructions for the prompt: rounded shapes, horizontals and warm light for safety; diagonals, pointed shapes and a small hero for tension ([Virginia Tech summary](https://www.itma.vt.edu/courses/visual/lesson_3_2.html)). Frightening things are better told in a light sentence than drawn, so the image prompt for such a scene should show the reaction. The image prompt needs the same content ceiling as the text. Since an adult co-reader outperforms any enhancement, the app should offer the adult an optional talk prompt on some pages and not present browser read-aloud as a replacement for a person. On representation, the notes found Rudine Sims Bishop's "mirrors, windows and sliding glass doors" framing ([Facing History reprint](https://www.facinghistory.org/resource-library/mirrors-windows-sliding-glass-doors)) but no sourced guidance on stereotype risks in generated characters; varying the hero by default and describing characters by specific visual traits is design reasoning only.

## Stories teach when the idea drives the plot

### Persuasion effects are real, small and work by lowering resistance

Meta-analyses agree that stories shift beliefs, attitudes and intentions by a small amount. Braddock and Dillard found correlations of about r = .17 to .23 across 5 to 40 studies per outcome ([Braddock and Dillard 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555)). Oschatz and Marker found a narrative advantage over non-narrative messages of d = .14 that persisted over delays of up to six months, though at delay it was not reliable for fictional narratives ([Oschatz and Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)). Almost all of this evidence is from adults and health topics; the notes found no meta-analysis of narrative persuasion in children. The story features that raise absorption are identifiable characters, an imaginable plot and believability ([van Laer et al. 2014](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/the-extended-transportation-imagery-model-a-meta-analysis-of-the-/)). Similarity of situation and goals matters more than demographic similarity ([Huang, Fung and Sun 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-023-04842-4)). Stories persuade partly because absorbed readers argue back less, and a visible intent to persuade removes that effect ([summary of Moyer-Gusé and Nabi](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/07CABFC5ABA0D3A6A369FCDDB21F8F76/core-reader)). The popular claim that fiction builds empathy is weak: the best experimental estimates are about g = .14 to .16, and a 2023 randomised study found none ([Wimmer et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38602788/); [Dodell-Feder et al. 2023](https://labsites.rochester.edu/scplab/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/The_impact_of_fiction_reading_.pdf)). DreamWeaver should not market itself on empathy.

Young children present the opposite problem from adults: they often miss the moral. Children did not reliably extract a story's theme until about age ten, according to a later paper's summary of Narvaez and colleagues ([Walker and Lombrozo 2017](https://cognition.princeton.edu/document/131)). In that later study, five- and six-year-olds prompted to explain why story events happened identified the lesson better than those asked to report events, and the authors say explaining is sometimes more effective than being told the moral ([Walker and Lombrozo](https://cognition.princeton.edu/publications/explaining-moral-story)). One study found sharing rose in four- to six-year-olds after a story with human characters and not after the same story with animal characters; a conceptual replication exists whose results the notes did not read ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/choosing-dora-over-franklin-u-t-study-says-children-learn-more-human-characters-not-animals)). "Don't preach" therefore means two things. For older readers, keep the verdict out of the narrator's mouth. For under-tens, have a character ask a "why do you think...?" question and then present the same idea in a new situation.

### Narrative helps memory, but story bolted onto content does not

Narrative text is remembered and understood better than expository text, g = .55 across 150 effect sizes, with extreme unexplained heterogeneity and signs of publication bias; listening did as well as reading, which supports narration ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). That finding is about genre. It does not show that wrapping a lesson in a story helps. In a meta-analysis of learning games, games with no story or a thin story outperformed those with medium story depth, a moderator the authors call tentative ([Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)). College students who played the narrative game Crystal Island learned less than those shown a matched slideshow, a result the notes reached through a summary ([ATD summary of Adams et al. 2012](https://www.td.org/insights/why-games-dont-teach)). Interesting but irrelevant "seductive details" reliably reduce learning, and more so when placed beside the relevant content ([WSU release on Sundararajan and Adesope 2020](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)); the notes could not retrieve the pooled effect size. The best-controlled positive evidence is Zombie Division: children aged 7 to 11 learned more division when it was the game's core mechanic than when the same game delivered it as quizzes between levels, and in a free-choice study of 16 children they played the integrated version seven times longer ([Habgood and Ainsworth 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556)). That is one game with small samples.

The rule for lesson stories is that the concept's causal chain must be the plot's causal chain. In DreamWeaver the core mechanic is picking a choice, so the reader should need the idea to choose well, and the story's outcome is the feedback. The anti-pattern is a quiz card between scenes or a narrator who pauses to explain and resumes unchanged.

### Learning techniques that fit the choice format

The learning-science techniques differ in strength. Practice testing and spaced practice are rated high-utility across ages ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html)). Prompting learners to explain yields g = .55, more with questions than commands ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)). Refutation texts, which state a misconception, say it is wrong and explain why, beat standard text by g = 0.41 ([Schroeder and Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z)). Explanatory feedback and scaffolding beat bare success-or-fail signals ([Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)). Letting learners attempt a problem before instruction helps older learners (g = 0.36) but **reverses for second to fifth graders**, who do better with instruction first ([Sinha and Kapur 2021](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4KnMgZq7)). Curiosity is strongest when the gap in knowledge feels small and closable, and adults remember answers better in a curious state ([review, PMC6891259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmc6891259)). Analogies help when the relations map and mislead when learners carry over surface features, so teaching models include a step that says where the analogy breaks down ([CSUN summary of Glynn](https://www.csun.edu/science/books/sourcebook/chapters/10-analogies/teaching-analogies.html)). Evidence on human-like language about animals is split, with one study finding it reduced young children's factual learning and another finding no harm ([Ganea et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989584); [Geerdts et al. 2016 summary](https://eepro.naaee.org/research/eeresearch/learning-about-real-animals-anthropomorphic-media)). Evidence on fantasy settings is mixed and domain-dependent; reviewers recommend restating facts in generic real-world language and talking with the child about what is true ([Strouse, Nyhout and Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)). Almost none of these techniques has been tested inside narrative fiction, so applying them to story choices is an inference.

### What a lesson pack should contain

The pack fields named in the brief (key ideas, misconceptions, vocabulary, checks for understanding) are necessary but not enough, because they are facts without situations. Each key idea should come with a story situation in which the idea has consequences, a short worked demonstration by a mentor character (required for readers under about eleven, given the productive-failure reversal), and a statement in generic real-world language for a character to say once. Each misconception should come with a tempting wrong choice, the consequence the story shows, and a refutation line a character speaks; the story then offers the choice again in a fresh situation, never a dead end. Each analogy should list what maps, what does not, and the misconception it is likely to create. Vocabulary gets one in-context use at the moment the word does work in the plot. Checks for understanding become two or three later scenes where a character asks the reader to recall or use the idea, plus one "why do you think that worked?" question and one transfer situation with different surface features. On a concept beat, the illustration should depict the concept and the scene should hold one idea.

Two safeguards belong in the design. The narrator should draw facts only from the pack, and a second check should compare the narration's factual sentences with it, because children carry story content, including errors, into their beliefs ([Ganea et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989584)). The adult should see what the story is meant to teach on its cover page while the story itself stays free of sermons. The notes found no peer-reviewed ethics framework for narrative persuasion aimed at children, and did not research regulation for children's apps, so both need separate checking.

## Measured LLM failures point at design, then model, then effort

### What language models get wrong in fiction

The measured weaknesses match DreamWeaver's exposure. Tian and colleagues found that LLM stories had happier, less complex arcs, fewer setbacks, earlier turning points and weaker climaxes than human stories, using GPT-4-era models ([Tian et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13248v2)). A 2026 study of ten models found their stories more similar to each other than human stories, with frontier models converging on a generic narrative, and found that negative prompting and temperature changes "fail to meaningfully address" this (abstract only) ([arXiv 2606.17350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17350)). Surface tics are extreme in open-weight models: the name "Elara" appeared 85,513 times more often in one model's creative writing than in human text ([Paech et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)). Models play villains less faithfully than good characters, an effect the authors attribute to safety training ([Yi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04962v1)). Even frontier agents with outlining, review and revision produced novels that readers found weak in long-range coherence ([AutoFiction](https://genaicreativity.org/icml2026/files/22/22_paper.pdf)). No study in the notes covers gpt-6-luna or any small late-2026 model.

The softening works in the product's favour for safety. The correction needed is toward real obstacles within stated limits: things get lost, plans fail, a rival outwits the hero, time runs out, a friend is let down. The ceiling should be stated in the prompt (peril without injury detail, fear resolved within the story). Research on children's story safety reports no agreed taxonomy or benchmark for frightening content ([arXiv 2502.12552](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12552v1)), and one pipeline pairs generation with a classifier that sends flagged segments back for rewriting ([PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11675295/)). DreamWeaver accepts typed actions, so reader input is a risk surface, and a separate check on input, narration and image prompt is safer than one prompt asked to supply both excitement and restraint. The notes did not verify OpenAI's current moderation tooling or policies for products aimed at minors.

### Planning has the strongest support, and it is cheap here

Planning ahead is the most consistently supported intervention. Outline-first systems beat direct generation in human evaluations: DOC gained 22.5 percentage points on plot coherence and 20.7 on interestingness over its predecessor, on older models with many calls per passage ([DOC, ACL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.190)). Giving the model arc information raised diversity by about 45%, and adding turning points raised suspense and engagement by about 40%, on GPT-4-era models ([Tian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13248v2)). Research on LLM interactive stories reports that such systems "struggle with pacing, structure, and narrative progression" and steers them with pre-authored storylets ([Dramamancer](https://mkremins.github.io/publications/Dramamancer_I3D2026.pdf)). The commercial engine Hidden Door builds on pre-computed tropes and story-thread templates, by the company's account in the press ([Yahoo-syndicated article](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/how-do-you-prevent-an-ai-generated-game-from-losing-the-plot-170002788.html)). No controlled study tests planning in a one-call-per-turn, reader-driven setting with a small model.

The transferable part is a hidden plan held in state. One call at story start writes the protagonist's want, the stake, the opposing force and its aim, two or three pending complications, one secret with its answer, a thematic question, an arc shape and a target length. The plan is a loose set of pending complications, not an outline, so reader choices stay free. For variety, the supported levers are structural: varied personas removed the diversity loss in one replication the notes could not fully verify ([arXiv 2502.17962](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.17962)), and arc conditioning raised diversity. The application should therefore pick the arc shape, tone, complication type, an unusual setting detail and character names from curated lists in code and pass them to the planning call. A code-side check can replace stock names. Expect that to fix surface repetition and not plot sameness.

### Memory is not the constraint; missing forward state is

Shipping products use layered memory: always-present essentials, a rolling summary and recent turns verbatim ([AI Dungeon memory system](https://help.aidungeon.com/faq/the-memory-system)). DreamWeaver's bible plus eight scenes is that pattern in miniature. Context size no longer forces it: gpt-6-luna has a 1,050,000-token window, with input at $0.10 per million tokens, cached input at $0.01 and output at $0.50 ([OpenAI model page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-6-luna.md)). A 60-scene story is roughly 7,000 tokens of narration by the notes' estimate, so sending every scene verbatim costs about $0.0007 per call uncached and far less with a stable, cacheable prefix. That removes most forgetting, and the 120-word summary can then stop serving as memory. What the bible lacks is forward-looking state: the plan, the phase and scene count, the trouble level, planted details with their answers, each character's want and attitude toward the hero, and threads written as fronts. Fixed appearance text should stay as it is for image consistency, with these mutable fields kept separate from it. No study compares summary-plus-window against full history for short interactive stories.

### Model choice and reasoning effort

Leaderboards agree that large models write better short fiction than small ones and disagree on almost everything else. Two main boards "nearly invert" each other's rankings, neither ranks a budget model near the top, and neither tests interactive, all-ages, 2-5 sentence narration ([Digital Applied analysis](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/which-ai-model-writes-best-leaderboards-disagree)). No benchmark result for gpt-6-luna was found. A bigger model does not by itself fix sameness, since frontier models converged on a generic narrative in the 2026 study. Expert and lay readers also diverge: with prompting alone, experts preferred human writing in 82.7% of comparisons while lay judges preferred the AI text ([Chakrabarty and Dhillon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18353)). DreamWeaver's readers are lay readers and children, so plot shape and surprise deserve the effort before sentence polish.

**Reasoning effort is the least-supported lever.** One 2026 paper reports that reasoning models show "limited gains" on open-ended writing (abstract only) ([R2-Write](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03004)), and no study in the notes measures effort against story quality or gives latency figures for Luna. OpenAI's general guidance is to start new integrations at medium effort, and that guide does not cover creative writing or the Luna tier ([OpenAI latest-model guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model)). Reasoning tokens are billed as output, by a third-party explainer ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gpt-6-luna-pricing)). The plausible benefit of medium effort is better bookkeeping, meaning honouring the plan and updating the bible, and its cost is delay before the first streamed word. A cheaper substitute is a short logged planning field of a few dozen tokens. Field order matters because earlier fields condition later ones; the evidence on order is blogs and small studies ([Castillo](https://dylancastillo.co/posts/llm-pydantic-order-matters.html)). A planning field before the narration delays streaming slightly. The alternative that keeps narration first is a "next beat" note written at the end of each turn and read at the start of the next, an untested adaptation of the RecurrentGPT pattern ([arXiv 2305.13304](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13304)). Developers have reported structured outputs emitting keys in an unexpected order, so the emitted order should be verified ([OpenAI community thread](https://community.openai.com/t/key-ordering-issue-in-openai-structured-outputs-despite-specified-sequence-in-json-schema/995040.md)). The notes found no study of JSON output against prose quality in fiction.

Self-critique and reranking are weakly supported for a 2-5 sentence output. The best zero-shot LLM judge agreed with human story preferences 73% of the time, and small judges did worse ([LitBench](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.362)), so a Luna critic can select for the wrong thing while doubling latency. The answer to the brief's question is an order of expected payoff: design changes first (plan, phase tracking, richer state, code-injected variety), a stronger model for the once-per-story planning call and perhaps the ending second, a stronger model on every turn third, and higher reasoning effort last. The first tier has the best evidence and the lowest cost. The last has almost no evidence and a visible latency cost.

### Vendor prompting guidance and how to evaluate changes

Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic publishes fiction-specific guidance that the notes could find. Anthropic's general guidance favours positive instructions with reasons, a one-sentence role, and three to five varied examples ([Anthropic prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). OpenAI's tips for a larger GPT-6 model include a short blocklist of overused phrases, as reported in the press ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-shares-prompting-tips-for-gpt-6-astra-including-a-blocklist-of-slop-words/)). Fixed example scenes can become a new source of sameness across thousands of stories, so rotating them from a pool is prudent; that is reasoning, not a measured result.

Evaluation should rest on human judgment. A workable protocol for one developer is to fix 10 to 20 premises and a scripted choice policy so variants can be replayed, generate 10 to 20 turns per variant, and compare whole transcripts blind and pairwise. Cheap counters track the documented failures: scenes before the first real setback, the fraction of scenes ending on an open question, repeated names and phrases across 50 stories, whether the ending references an early planted detail, and how different the three choices are. Product telemetry (scenes per story, when readers press the close button, replays, the scene at which readers abandon) is better evidence for this audience than anything in the craft literature.

## Conclusion

The notes change the question the brief asked. "Which model, and how much thinking?" assumes the narrator's weakness is its prose. The evidence says the weakness is shape: a narrator that continues with the likeliest next scene will resolve trouble early, keep everyone safe and tell the same story twice, and a larger model does the same thing with better sentences. DreamWeaver's architecture already contains the remedy in outline. The story bible is a state machine waiting for forward-looking fields, the three choices are a pacing instrument, and the application code can do what the model does badly, which is count scenes, hold a tension target and roll dice for variety. The narrator model then has a narrower job that a small model can plausibly do: write one good beat toward a stated purpose.

Two cautions carry forward. The craft rules are convergent opinion and the interactive-design rules are practitioner experience, so the app's own blind comparisons and telemetry will be better evidence for its readers than the sources behind this report. And the lesson packs raise the stakes: the learning research is the firmest material here, and its central finding is that a story wrapped around content can teach less than a plain explanation. Educational stories should be held to a learning check, not an engagement number, before any claim is made for them.
