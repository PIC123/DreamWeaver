# Story craft fundamentals (for DreamWeaver: short, turn-based, AI-narrated stories)

Research date: 2026-11-23. Evidence labels used throughout:

- **[EMPIRICAL]** backed by published research (strength noted)
- **[CRAFT CONSENSUS]** widely shared by practitioners and teachers, not experimentally tested
- **[CONTESTED]** disputed, culturally specific, or criticised as overused

Sourcing caveat for the report writer: many canonical craft books (McKee, Truby, Yorke, Swain, Le Guin's *Steering the Craft*, Saunders, Salesses) are not freely readable online. Where I could only reach reviews or summaries of them, I say so. Anything I know from background knowledge but could not verify against a source this session is confined to the "Gaps" sections and marked as unverified.

## 1. Structure models: what each claims, what it is good for, main criticisms

### Takeaway
Every popular structure model is a description of one family of stories that later got promoted to a universal template; the durable, transferable core across them is small: causally linked events, a change of fortune, and an ending that follows from what came before. The only model with direct quantitative support is the "emotional arc" idea (Vonnegut, tested by Reagan et al. 2016), and even that study describes sentiment trajectories in old English-language novels, not what makes a story good.

### Cited Findings

**Aristotle / three-act structure [CRAFT CONSENSUS for causality; the "three acts" label is later]**
- Aristotle's requirement is that each event be the "necessary or probable" result of what preceded it; reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis) must arise from the plot itself, not be added on. A reversal is surprising yet appears as a necessary outcome; the best plots make reversal and recognition simultaneous. A well-constructed plot "must neither begin nor end haphazardly". — [SparkNotes summary of Poetics ch. 10-12](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [Univ. of Hawaii CriticaLink guide to Poetics XI](https://english.hawaii.edu/criticalink/aristotle/gloss/gloss11.html); [Toronto Metropolitan Univ. Pressbooks edition of the Poetics, ch. 11](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/thepoetics/chapter/11/)
- John Yorke (*Into the Woods*, 2013) presents five acts as "merely a detailed refinement" of three-act structure (two extra breaks inside act two), with a midpoint at which the protagonist typically has a transformative revelation, and symmetry around that midpoint. He also argues the competing structure theories largely map onto one another. Sceptical reviewers dispute some of his act-break placements. — [The Script Blog on Yorke](https://thescriptblog.com/on-john-yorkes-into-the-woods); [Kirkus review](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/john-yorke/into-the-woods-five-act/print/); [B. Gourley review](https://berniegourley.com/tag/five-act-structure/)

**Freytag's pyramid [CONTESTED as a general model]**
- Gustav Freytag published *Die Technik des Dramas* in 1863 (English 1894). The sequence is exposition, rising action, climax/peripeteia, falling action, denouement, described as a tension curve over a five-act drama. — [Utrecht University Dramaturgy Database](https://dramaturgydatabase.hum.uu.nl/record/die-technik-des-dramas/); [Wikipedia: Gustav Freytag](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Freytag)
- It was an analysis of ancient Greek and Shakespearean drama (tragedy in particular), not a theory of stories in general; the climax sits in the middle, which does not match most modern fiction where the climax is near the end. — [Univ. of Geneva TECFA narrative wiki: Five-act model](https://tecfalabs.unige.ch/mediawiki-narrative/index.php/Five-act_model); full text at [Internet Archive](https://ia600208.us.archive.org/25/items/freytagstechniqu00freyuoft/freytagstechniqu00freyuoft.pdf)

**Hero's Journey (Campbell 1949; Vogler's Hollywood adaptation) [CONTESTED as universal; useful as one quest template]**
- Claim: a single "monomyth" pattern (departure, initiation, return) underlies hero myths worldwide.
- Classicists Joel Christensen and Sarah E. Bond (LA Review of Books, 12 Aug 2021) argue Campbell "cherry-picked" evidence and fitted global myths to a preset frame; that the model is "nearly perfectly narcissistic" in centring one protagonist; and that it reflects a masculine, individualist viewpoint. They report Brett M. Rogers' point that *Star Wars* and the PBS series *The Power of Myth* validated the pattern culturally, so people then saw it everywhere. Patrice Rankine gives partial credit: Campbell elevated non-Western myth, but flattened its complexity. — [LARB: "The Man Behind the Myth"](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-man-behind-the-myth-should-we-question-the-heros-journey)
- Folklorist Alan Dundes (2004 presidential plenary to the American Folklore Society, published in *Journal of American Folklore* 2005) reportedly called Campbell a "non-expert" relying on secondhand material and said universal-archetype claims harm folklore study; Barre Toelken reportedly criticised Campbell for citing only stories that fit. Wikipedia summarises that folklorists have dismissed the concept as non-scholarly. These attributions come via secondary sources and were not checked against the originals. — [Wikipedia: Hero's journey](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Monomyth); [Patheos, "Why Folklorists Hate Joseph Campbell's Work" (blog by a folklorist)](https://patheos.com/blogs/foxyfolklorist/why-folklorists-hate-joseph-campbells-work)
- Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" (1986) argues the hero/conflict story is only one shape: a story can be a container holding many things in relation, with conflict as just one of the contents. "The hero does not look well in this bag... You put him in a bag and he looks like a rabbit, like a potato." — [Le Guin essay text](https://wiki.xxiivv.com/docs/carrier_bag.html); commentary: [Ploughshares blog](https://pshares.org/blog/a-carrier-bag-theory-of-revolution/)

**Dan Harmon's Story Circle [CRAFT CONSENSUS as a compact tool; inherits Campbell's limits]**
- Eight steps: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change. Written as a tutorial for Channel 101, Harmon's short-film festival, to help people tell a complete story in about five minutes; later used on *Community* and *Rick and Morty*. It is a compressed descendant of the Hero's Journey. — [Channel 101 wiki: "Story Structure 101: Super Basic Shit" by Dan Harmon](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit); [StudioBinder summary](https://studiobinder.com/blog/dan-harmon-story-circle)

**Save the Cat (Blake Snyder, 2005) [CONTESTED: effective scaffold, criticised as formula]**
- A 15-beat sheet prescribing what should happen at specific page counts of a screenplay. Peter Suderman (Slate, July 2013, "Save the Movie!") argued that Hollywood blockbusters feel interchangeable because they follow it page by page. Slate notes Snyder insisted the beat sheet is "a structure, not a formula". A counter-argument is that these beats were widely known before 2005, so the book codified rather than caused the sameness. — [Slate article](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/culturebox/2013/07/hollywood_and_blake_snyder_s_screenwriting_book_save_the_cat.html); [Reason summary by the author's outlet](https://reason.com/2013/07/19/peter-suderman-in-slate-on-hollywoods-se/); [No Film School, 25 Jul 2013](https://nofilmschool.com/2013/07/screenwriter-reliance-story-structure-ruined-movies)

**Kishotenketsu [CRAFT CONSENSUS as a real tradition; the "no conflict" framing is CONTESTED]**
- Four parts: ki (introduction), sho (development), ten (twist: an unexpected turn or new element, often the climax), ketsu (conclusion that reconciles the twist with what came before). Originates in Chinese four-line poetry (*qichengzhuanhe*; described by Fan Heng, 1272-1330), with Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese forms. Used in four-panel (yonkoma) manga, essays, and Nintendo level design (*Super Mario Galaxy*, *Super Mario 3D World*). — [Wikipedia: Kishotenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- The popular "plot without conflict" claim comes from a 2012 blog post by Still Eating Oranges. It is more accurate to say the form does not *centre* a driving conflict: kishotenketsu narratives "can and often do contain conflict" (e.g. *Parasite*). Interest is generated by the twist and by the reader working out how it connects. — [Still Eating Oranges post, mirrored](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/8044203-the-significance-of-plot-without-conflict?tab=book); [The Rumpus, Mar 2013](https://therumpus.net/2013/03/can-you-tell-a-story-without-conflict); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); academic treatment: [TEXT journal, "Kishotenketsu and its potential applications to prose writing"](https://textjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/146391-kishotenketsu-and-its-potential-applications-to-prose-writing/attachment/308900.pdf)

**Vonnegut's story shapes and Reagan et al. 2016 [EMPIRICAL, with limits]**
- Vonnegut plotted stories on a good-fortune/ill-fortune axis over time: "Man in a Hole" (starts fairly well, dips, ends a little higher than the start; "it doesn't need to be a man and it doesn't need to be a hole"), "Boy Meets Girl", "Cinderella". He noted that *Hamlet* resists classification because we cannot tell whether events are good or bad news. His University of Chicago master's thesis on this was rejected (by his account in *Palm Sunday*, for being too simple and too much fun). — [Open Culture, 2019](https://www.openculture.com/2019/12/why-the-university-of-chicago-rejected-kurt-vonneguts-masters-thesis.html); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/27888/kurt-vonneguts-story-diagrams); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/culture-religion/vonnegut-shapes)
- Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth and Dodds (EPJ Data Science, 2016) ran sentiment analysis over a filtered set of English fiction from Project Gutenberg and, using matrix decomposition plus supervised and unsupervised learning, found "six core emotional arcs which form the essential building blocks of complex emotional trajectories". Abstract: "particular emotional arcs enjoy greater success, as measured by downloads." — [arXiv abstract 1606.07772](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772); [journal page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0093-1)
- The six arcs: rags to riches (rise), tragedy (fall), man in a hole (fall-rise), Icarus (rise-fall), Cinderella (rise-fall-rise), Oedipus (fall-rise-fall). Corpus filter: English fiction, 20,000-100,000 words, more than 40 downloads. — [Authors' online appendix, Univ. of Vermont](https://www.uvm.edu/storylab/share/papers/reagan2016b/index.html); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772)
- Corpus size discrepancy: the latest arXiv version (v3, 26 Sep 2016) says 1,327 stories; the first preprint (v1) says 1,737. Use 1,327 unless the journal text says otherwise. — [arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772v3); [arXiv v1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772v1.pdf)

**Pixar's "22 rules" (Emma Coats) [CRAFT CONSENSUS; informal, not official Pixar doctrine]**
- Tweeted by former Pixar story artist Emma Coats as "story basics" (sources date the tweets to 2011 or 2012; inconsistently reported). The rules most usable for a narrator:
  - #4, the "story spine": "Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___."
  - #1: "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes."
  - #6: "What is your character good at, comfortable with? Throw the polar opposite at them."
  - #7: "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle."
  - #12: "Discount the 1st thing that comes to mind. And the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th - get the obvious out of the way."
  - #13: "Give your characters opinions. Passive/malleable might seem likable to you as you write, but it's poison to the audience."
  - #16: "What are the stakes? Give us reason to root for the character. What happens if they don't succeed? Stack the odds against."
  - #19: "Coincidences to get characters into trouble are great; coincidences to get them out of it are cheating."
  - #22: "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?"
  — [New York Film Academy, full list, 29 Oct 2014](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/); [Gizmodo/io9, 2012](https://gizmodo.com/the-22-rules-of-storytelling-according-to-pixar-5916970)

**Critique of treating any of these as neutral rules [CONTESTED territory, respected source]**
- Matthew Salesses (*Craft in the Real World*, Catapult, 2021) argues craft is not a neutral rulebook but the accumulated expectations of a dominant culture's audience, and questions whether fiction's insistence on cause-and-effect and individual agency is universal. Well reviewed (NYT Book Review rave by Laila Lalami; BookPage starred). — [Sydney Review of Books, "Making Craft Conscious"](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/review/making-craft-conscious); [Book Marks review roundup](https://bookmarks.reviews/reviews/all/craft-in-the-real-world-rethinking-fiction-writing-and-workshopping/); [Brooklyn Rail, Feb 2021](https://brooklynrail.org/2021/02/books/Matthew-Salessess-Craft-in-the-Real-World/)

### Inferences
- For DreamWeaver, structure should be chosen per story, not hard-coded. A sensible menu: story spine / Story Circle for quests and adventure (compact, five-minute origin fits 5-15 scenes); kishotenketsu for gentle, bedtime or slice-of-life stories for young children where a driving antagonist is unwanted; an emotional-arc target (e.g. man-in-a-hole, Cinderella) as a simple planning dial that works at any length.
- Models scale differently. The story spine and kishotenketsu compress to four to six scenes. Save the Cat's 15 page-locked beats and the full 12/17-stage Hero's Journey do not fit a 5-scene story and risk formulaic output at 60.
- Emotional arcs are the most "promptable" structure: a narrator can be told the intended fortune level for each stretch of the story (rising, falling, low point at about two thirds), independent of plot content.
- Reagan et al. tells us which sentiment shapes are common and correlate with downloads in public-domain novels. It does not show that imposing a shape makes a story better, and says nothing about interactive or children's fiction.
- Pixar rule #7 (know the ending first) is in tension with reader choice; the practical reading is to fix the *kind* of ending and the central question early while leaving the route open.

### Gaps
- I could not open the EPJ Data Science full text (redirect to login), so the statement of *which* arcs drew most downloads is not verified here. Unverified recollection: the paper reports Icarus, Oedipus and two sequential man-in-a-hole arcs as most downloaded. Check the paper before quoting.
- Did not verify Dundes's 2005 JAF article or Toelken's text directly; attributions are secondary.
- Vogler's *The Writer's Journey* (the 12-stage Hollywood memo/book) was not sourced this session; nor Maureen Murdock's *Heroine's Journey* or Maria Tatar's critique, which LARB only links to.
- No primary text for McKee's *Story* or Truby's 22 steps was reached. Truby is a prominent critic of three-act structure as mechanical (unverified background knowledge).
- The Jane Alison (*Meander, Spiral, Explode*) critique of the arc/pyramid was only seen via an unreliable aggregator; not cited above.
- No empirical research found testing whether beat-sheet conformity improves audience response.

## 2. Character: want vs need, flaws, arcs, agency, why readers care

### Takeaway
The strongest shared principle is that readers attach to characters who actively want something and make choices under pressure, more than to characters who are merely likeable or successful. Want/need and "lie the character believes" are useful planning shorthand but are screenwriting conventions, not laws, and respected practitioners question them.

### Cited Findings
- **Want vs need [CRAFT CONSENSUS in screenwriting; CONTESTED]**: Truby's *Anatomy of Story* treats "weakness and need" as the first thing to establish (an internal flaw the character must overcome), with "desire" as the external goal the audience tracks. — [Stage 32 blog quoting Truby](https://www.stage32.com/blog/2782). Screenwriter John August argues that fixating on want versus need creates a false framework that does not help write better scenes. — [johnaugust.com, "Rethinking motivation" (2008)](https://johnaugust.com/2008/rethinking-motivation). Script Factory warns a stark want/need split often produces characters who are hard to engage with. — [Script Factory](https://www.scriptfactory.co.uk/blog/want-vs-need-is-the-distinction-necessary). Dramatica notes the two can resolve independently (goal achieved or not; personal problem resolved or not), giving four ending types. — [Dramatica](https://dramatica.com/blog/mapping-character-want-and-need-across-story-systems)
- **Arc types [CRAFT CONSENSUS; this taxonomy is one teacher's]**: K.M. Weiland's widely used scheme: positive change (character starts believing a Lie, comes to accept the Truth), flat (character already holds the Truth, is tested by doubt, and changes the world around them), negative (disillusionment, fall, corruption). — [Weiland, Helping Writers Become Authors: types of arc](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/learn-5-types-of-character-arc-at-a-glance/); [Weiland on flat arcs and doubt](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/doubt-key-flat-character-arcs/)
- **Effort over success**: "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes" (Pixar #1). **Opinions and activity**: "Passive/malleable... is poison to the audience" (#13). **Test the comfort zone**: throw the polar opposite of their strength at them (#6). **Honest emotion**: "If you were your character, in this situation, how would you feel? Honesty lends credibility to unbelievable situations" (#15). — [NYFA list](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Failure builds empathy**: the Writing Excuses podcast (Sanderson, Kowal, Wells, Tayler and others) argues that characters failing spectacularly builds tension and reader empathy. — [Writing Excuses 10.29, "Why Should My Characters Fail Spectacularly?"](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/)
- **[EMPIRICAL] Transportation and identification**: Green and Brock (2000, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) introduced "transportation" (absorption into a story world) and a scale to measure it. Identification (experiencing the story through a character's perspective) is a related but distinct mechanism; transportation correlates with identification and liking for protagonists. The research mostly measures persuasion/belief change rather than enjoyment, and reviews note several studies (including Green and Brock's own experiments 1-3) failed to manipulate transportation successfully. — [Review in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6999344); [Wikipedia: Transportation theory](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology)); [Green & Appel 2024 preprint, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)
- **Carrier-bag / non-heroic protagonists**: Le Guin's essay supports stories about gatherers, groups and relationships rather than a single conquering hero. — [Le Guin](https://wiki.xxiivv.com/docs/carrier_bag.html)

### Inferences
- In DreamWeaver the reader's three choices *are* the protagonist's agency. The narrator's job is to make each choice express character (a want, a value, a fear), not just a direction, and to make the consequences visibly follow from that choice.
- Full positive-change arcs need room; they are realistic at roughly 20+ scenes. For 5-10 scene stories a flat arc (a steadfast character whose value is tested once and holds, changing those around them) or a single small realisation is achievable and common in picture books.
- A workable minimal character spec for the narrator: one concrete want, one trait or opinion that colours how they act, one thing they are afraid of or bad at. State the want in scene 1.
- "Trying more than succeeding" translates directly: let the protagonist's attempts cost effort and sometimes partially fail, and narrate the effort.

### Gaps
- McKee's line that true character is revealed by choices made under pressure is widely quoted but I could not source it this session (unverified background knowledge; *Story*, 1997).
- Blake Snyder's "save the cat" likeability device itself (an early sympathetic act) was not sourced directly.
- No empirical study was found that tests want/need, flaws or arc type against reader enjoyment.
- No source reached on child-audience character identification specifically (likely covered by another researcher).

## 3. Conflict, stakes and tension

### Takeaway
The single most transferable rule is causal linkage: each beat should connect to the next by "therefore" or "but", never "and then". Around it sit complementary tools (stakes, escalation, try-fail with "yes, but / no, and", scene-and-sequel) that are practitioner wisdom rather than tested findings, plus a credible minority position that conflict need not be the engine at all.

### Cited Findings
- **Therefore / but [CRAFT CONSENSUS, very widely adopted]**: Trey Parker and Matt Stone, guest-lecturing in Ken Liotti's "Story-Telling Strategies" class at NYU Tisch (filmed for mtvU's *Stand In*; most outlets date it 2011), said that if the words "and then" belong between your story beats you have a problem; every beat should be joined by "therefore" or "but", which is what creates cause and effect. — [Cracked write-up](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html); [TheWrap, 8 Sep 2011 (confirms the visit and date; quotes Parker "simple was better")](https://thewrap.com/south-park-creators-visit-nyu-class-lessons-storytelling-30806); [HuffPost](https://huffingtonpost.co.uk/entry/south-park-nyu_n_954419)
- This matches Aristotle's "necessary or probable" sequence and Pixar's "Because of that... Because of that..." spine. — [SparkNotes Poetics](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Causality and escalation (George Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*, 2021) [CRAFT CONSENSUS]**: "The queen died, and the king died of grief" turns sequence into meaning. Escalation is "a meaningful alteration in the terms of the story"; "a swath of prose earns its place in the story to the extent that it contributes to our sense that the story is (still) escalating." "Always be escalating" means refusing to repeat beats: a recurring element's second appearance is an opportunity for variation. A story works by small pulses, each setting an expectation that the next meets or alters. — [The Marginalian excerpt and commentary, 30 Sep 2021](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/); [New Statesman review, Feb 2021](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/2021/02/swim-pond-rain-george-saunders-review)
- **Try-fail cycles and "yes, but / no, and" [CRAFT CONSENSUS in genre fiction]**: "Yes, but" = the character succeeds or moves toward the goal, with a complication. "No, and" = they fail and the situation worsens. Writing Excuses frames these as controls on momentum, uses the opening of *Raiders of the Lost Ark* as a run of yes-buts ending in a no-and, and warns against "checklist mentality" (e.g. always three cycles); the number should fit the story. — [Writing Excuses 10.29](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/); [Writing Excuses try-fail episode](https://writingexcuses.com/?p=22664)
- **Scene and sequel (Dwight Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965) [CRAFT CONSENSUS in commercial fiction]**: a scene is "a unit of conflict", an effort to attain a goal despite opposition: goal, conflict, disaster. A sequel is the bridge: reaction, dilemma, decision; the decision becomes the next scene's goal. Sequel "translates disaster into goal" and controls tempo. — [Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Scene_and_sequel); [Randy Ingermanson, Advanced Fiction Writing](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/?p=75); [Writers Helping Writers, 2022](https://writershelpingwriters.net/2022/03/scenes-vs-sequels-whats-a-good-balance/)
- **Stakes**: "What happens if they don't succeed? Stack the odds against" (Pixar #16); coincidence may cause trouble but must not solve it (#19). — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Conflict is not mandatory [CONTESTED]**: kishotenketsu generates interest through a twist and its reconciliation rather than a central confrontation; Le Guin treats conflict as one of many things a story can hold; Salesses treats conflict-centred plot as a culturally specific expectation. — [Wikipedia: Kishotenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); [Le Guin](https://wiki.xxiivv.com/docs/carrier_bag.html); [Sydney Review of Books on Salesses](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/review/making-craft-conscious)
- **[EMPIRICAL, mixed] Suspense is not the only source of pleasure**: Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011, *Psychological Science*) found readers rated spoiled short stories as more enjoyable across three genres, suggesting outcome uncertainty matters less than assumed; a follow-up proposed processing fluency as the mechanism. Later studies conflict: at least one found spoilers reduced enjoyment, another found no effect on overall enjoyment but lower suspense and "movingness". — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html); [Univ. of California summary](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/spoiler-alert-spoilers-make-you-enjoy-stories-more); [original paper PDF](https://csus.edu/indiv/m/merlinos/pdf/psychological%20science-2011-leavitt-1152-4.pdf); [W. Levine lab, Univ. of Arkansas (non-replication)](https://wordpressua.uark.edu/whlevine/?p=122)

### Inferences
- Scene-and-sequel maps almost one to one onto DreamWeaver's loop. Narrator text = the outcome of the last choice (yes-but / no-and) plus a beat of reaction; the three choices = the dilemma; the reader's pick = the decision that becomes the next goal. This is the highest-value mapping in these notes.
- A concrete narrator instruction: "Begin each scene with a consequence of the reader's last choice (therefore) or a complication to it (but). Never begin with an unrelated new event." This can be checked mechanically in review.
- Use "yes, but" most of the time for young children (progress with a wrinkle) and reserve "no, and" for older audiences or for the low point of the arc; unbroken failure is discouraging, unbroken success is flat.
- "Always be escalating / never repeat a beat" is a direct guard against a known LLM failure: producing a string of similar encounters. Instruct the narrator that each scene must change something (what is known, what is at risk, or a relationship).
- Stakes scale down: for a five-year-old, "the kite will blow away before the picnic" is a full stake. Stakes must be stated once, concretely, early.
- The spoiler research mildly supports telling the reader what kind of story they are in (promises) and re-reading/replaying; it does not justify removing uncertainty from individual choices.

### Gaps
- No verbatim transcript of the Parker/Stone clip was fetched; wording above is paraphrase from press coverage. Date variously given as 2011, 2014, 2017; 2011 is best supported.
- Swain's own wording was not consulted; summaries only.
- "Types of conflict" (person vs person / self / nature / society / technology / fate) is a classroom taxonomy for which I found no authoritative origin this session; treat it as a teaching convenience rather than a principle (unverified background knowledge).
- Mary Robinette Kowal's MICE Quotient (useful for nesting and closing threads in order) was not researched.
- No empirical test found of try-fail cycles or "therefore/but" on engagement.

## 4. Setups and payoffs

### Takeaway
"Everything introduced is a promise" is among the oldest and least disputed craft ideas; the live dispute is about deferred mysteries, where critics argue that withheld answers create debt that grows with every scene. For a short interactive story this means: plant few things, track them, and pay them off.

### Cited Findings
- **Chekhov's gun [CRAFT CONSENSUS; wording uncertain]**: the principle is best sourced to Chekhov's letter to Aleksandr Lazarev of 1 Nov 1889 (do not put a loaded rifle on stage if no one is going to fire it; "it's wrong to make promises you don't mean to keep"). The familiar "rifle hanging on the wall in act one must go off" wording comes from reminiscences (Gurlyand, published 1904; Shchukin's memoirs, 1911), so exact wording varies. — [Wikipedia: Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun); [Gotham Writers](https://www.writingclasses.com/toolbox/ask-writer/whats-this-business-about-chekhovs-gun)
- Criticism: applied rigidly the rule strips out texture and makes stories predictable because every detail becomes a signal. — [Writer's Digest, "Don't Shoot: Why I Don't Believe in Chekhov's Gun"](https://www.writersdigest.com/dont-shoot-why-i-dont-believe-in-chekhovs-gun)
- **Fair resolution**: Aristotle's necessity/probability and Pixar #19 (coincidence may start trouble, not end it) are the payoff-side of the same rule: the solution must come from material already in the story. — [SparkNotes Poetics](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Callbacks as escalation**: Saunders treats a recurring element's return as a chance for meaningful variation, which is what a callback is. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)
- **Mystery box [CONTESTED]**: J.J. Abrams's 2007 TED talk: "Mystery is the catalyst for imagination... maybe there are times when mystery is more important than knowledge." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/talks/j_j_abrams_the_mystery_box?language=en). Criticisms: it values mystery for its own sake rather than as motivation to solve it ([Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/a-mystery-box-full-of-red-matter-26806)); "the longer you tease something out, the bigger the payoff has to be", with *Lost* as the standard cautionary example ([Birth.Movies.Death, 2016](https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2016/01/12/we-created-the-mystery-box.html)); the term now denotes a whole genre of withheld-answer shows ([Wikipedia: Mystery box show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_box_show)); *The Cloverfield Paradox* cited as the low point ([AV Club](https://www.avclub.com/the-cloverfield-paradox-represents-the-nadir-of-j-j-ab-1823267791)). These are opinion pieces, not studies.
- **Sanderson's promises** link setups to endings: early scenes promise tone, genre, character arc and conflict type; see section 7. — [Odyssey Workshop podcast #135](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=22073)

### Inferences
- An LLM narrator writing scene by scene has no natural memory of what it has planted. A small explicit "open threads" list carried in the prompt (objects, questions, promises, named characters), with instructions to pay off or deliberately close each before the end, operationalises Chekhov's gun.
- Scale: a 5-scene story can carry one setup/payoff pair (an object or skill introduced in scene 1 or 2 that solves the problem at the end). A 30-60 scene story can carry three to five threads plus running callbacks. More than that will be dropped.
- Mysteries in an improvised story are especially risky because nobody has decided the answer. Rule of thumb for the narrator: do not raise a question unless an answer is decided (even privately) at the moment it is raised, and resolve it within a bounded number of scenes.
- For children, the callback (the return of a familiar phrase, creature or object) is itself a pleasure and costs one sentence.
- Not every detail must fire: one or two sensory details per scene can be pure texture. The promise rule applies to things the narration *emphasises*.

### Gaps
- No empirical research found on foreshadowing or payoff and reader satisfaction.
- Did not consult a scholarly edition of Chekhov's letters; wording should not be quoted as exact.
- "Red herring" and "plant and payoff" terminology in screenwriting manuals was not separately sourced.

## 5. Prose craft: show vs tell, specificity, pacing, voice, economy

### Takeaway
"Show, don't tell" is the most over-applied rule in craft teaching: its famous Chekhov quotation is apocryphal in that form, and critics treat it as a stylistic preference. The usable core is narrower: prefer one concrete, specific sensory detail to an abstract label for emotionally important moments, and summarise (tell) freely to move time and keep economy.

### Cited Findings
- The "Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass" line is not found in Chekhov in that form. Quote Investigator traces it to a paraphrase of a letter to his brother Alexander (May 1886) advising that a moonlit night be evoked by a detail such as a piece of broken bottle glittering on a mill dam. — [Quote Investigator, 30 Jul 2013](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2013/07/30/moon-glint/)
- Hemingway was a notable proponent of the style; his iceberg theory (omit what you know; the reader will feel it) is related but distinct. Critics describe "show, don't tell" as a stylistic preference rather than a rule. — [Wikipedia: Show, don't tell](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show,_don%27t_tell); [Scribophile Academy: "The show versus tell debate"](https://www.scribophile.com/academy/the-show-versus-tell-debate)
- Salesses's broader point applies: rules of this kind encode one tradition's tastes; oral and non-Western storytelling traditions tell freely. — [Sydney Review of Books](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/review/making-craft-conscious)
- **Economy [CRAFT CONSENSUS]**: "Simplify. Focus. Combine characters. Hop over detours" (Pixar #5); "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?" (#22). — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/). Saunders: every passage must earn its place by contributing to escalation. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/). Parker: "We started to learn that simple was better." — [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/south-park-creators-visit-nyu-class-lessons-storytelling-30806)
- **Avoid the obvious**: discount the first several ideas that come to mind (Pixar #12). — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Pacing**: Swain's sequel "telescopes reality" and "controls tempo": action units speed up, reaction units slow down. — [Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)
- **[EMPIRICAL, indirect] Ease of processing aids enjoyment**: the spoiler follow-up found that perceived ease of processing mediated enjoyment. — [Univ. of California summary](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/spoiler-alert-spoilers-make-you-enjoy-stories-more)

### Inferences
- A 2-5 sentence scene cannot dramatise everything. A workable budget: one sentence of consequence (telling is fine), one concrete sensory image (showing), one line of character action or speech, and a final sentence that opens onto the choice. That is the smallest unit that still contains change.
- With an illustration on every page, the picture carries much of the "showing". Narration should add what a picture cannot: sound, smell, touch, inner feeling, causality, time.
- Pixar #12 is directly relevant to LLMs, which default to the most probable (most clichéd) detail. Instruct the narrator to pick the second or third detail that comes to mind and to prefer a specific noun (a "dented brass kettle") over a generic one.
- For young children, naming an emotion plainly ("Mia felt brave") is developmentally appropriate telling; pair it with one physical sign rather than replacing it.
- Voice is best enforced by a per-story style line fixed at the start (person, tense, register, one or two signature habits) and reused every turn; drift is the main risk over 60 scenes.

### Gaps
- Le Guin's *Steering the Craft* (her defence of telling, and her chapters on rhythm, sentence length and "crowding and leaping") was not reachable online; nothing from it is cited. Unverified background knowledge: she explicitly objects to "show, don't tell" as an absolute and to conflict as the sole basis of story.
- No source consulted on read-aloud prosody or sentence rhythm for children's narration.
- Research on concreteness/imagery and reading comprehension exists (e.g. dual coding) but was not searched.

## 6. Theme

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree theme is discovered through what characters choose and what those choices cost, and is weakened by being announced; this is craft opinion with no empirical test, and fable traditions that state a moral outright are a legitimate exception, especially for children.

### Cited Findings
- "Trying for theme is important, but you won't see what the story is actually about til you're at the end of it. Now rewrite." (Pixar #3); "Why must you tell THIS story? What's the belief burning within you that your story feeds off of? That's the heart of it." (#14). — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- Saunders: meaning comes from causality. "The king died of grief" *means* the king loved the queen without anyone saying so. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)
- Weiland's Lie/Truth model makes theme the Truth the protagonist accepts (positive arc), upholds (flat) or rejects (negative): theme is carried by the arc. — [Helping Writers Become Authors](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/write-character-arcs/)
- Dramatica's outcome/judgment split shows how endings argue a theme: got the goal and resolved the inner problem, got one without the other, or neither. — [Dramatica](https://dramatica.com/blog/mapping-character-want-and-need-across-story-systems)
- Yorke frames the midpoint as a transformative revelation and the final act as "home again, changed". — [The Script Blog](https://thescriptblog.com/on-john-yorkes-into-the-woods); [B. Gourley review](https://berniegourley.com/tag/five-act-structure/)

### Inferences
- In an interactive story the reader's choices carry the theme. If choices repeatedly pose the same value tension (e.g. safety versus kindness, alone versus together) and consequences respond consistently, a theme emerges without a stated moral.
- Practical instruction: give the narrator a one-line thematic *question* (not an answer) at story start; make at least one choice per act a test of that question; let the ending reflect the pattern of the reader's choices.
- Avoid a closing moral sentence for older readers. For young children a gentle one-line reflection is in keeping with fable tradition, but it should name what the character did, not lecture.

### Gaps
- McKee's "controlling idea" and Truby's "moral argument" are the standard primary treatments and were not reachable; no direct citation.
- Lisa Cron's *Wired for Story* (2012) claims neuroscience support for story principles such as "story is about how the plot affects the protagonist internally". The one critical notice found, in The Scientist, flags her reliance on popular sources including Jonah Lehrer. I found no peer-reviewed evaluation; treat her science framing as popularisation, her craft advice as craft opinion. — [The Scientist capsule review](https://www.the-scientist.com/capsule-reviews-40-40525)
- No empirical studies on implicit versus explicit theme and reader response were found.

## 7. Endings

### Takeaway
A satisfying ending keeps the promises the opening made and resolves the story from materials already in play, while arriving by a route the reader did not predict. Sanderson's "promises, progress, payoff" is the most instruction-ready formulation and scales to any length.

### Cited Findings
- **Promises, progress, payoff [CRAFT CONSENSUS]**: Brandon Sanderson teaches that the opening makes promises (tone, genre, character arc, type of conflict/plot), the middle must show visible progress toward them, and the ending must pay them off; setting expectations early stops readers wondering whether the story is for them. In a series some promises may stay open as long as the first book concludes satisfyingly. Delivered at the Odyssey Writing Workshop (summer 2020) and as Lecture 2 of his 2025 BYU course (released 31 Jan 2025). — [Odyssey Podcast #135](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=22073); [2025 Lecture 2, podcast version](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2447316/episodes/16528169-promise-progress-payoff-plot-theory-brandon-sanderson-s-writing-lecture-2-2025)
- **Surprising but necessary**: Aristotle's reversal is contrary to expectation yet a necessary or probable outcome of what preceded it. — [SparkNotes Poetics](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [CriticaLink](https://english.hawaii.edu/criticalink/aristotle/gloss/gloss11.html)
- **No rescue from outside**: coincidences that get characters out of trouble "are cheating" (Pixar #19). **Plan it**: "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle... Endings are hard" (#7). — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Shape of the close**: Story Circle ends on Return and Change; Yorke's fifth act is "home again, changed"; the story spine ends "Until finally ___". — [Channel 101 wiki](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit); [The Script Blog](https://thescriptblog.com/on-john-yorkes-into-the-woods); [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/)
- **Kishotenketsu endings** reconcile the twist with the opening rather than declaring a winner, and may leave questions open. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); [The Rumpus](https://therumpus.net/2013/03/can-you-tell-a-story-without-conflict)
- **[EMPIRICAL] Arc endings**: in Reagan et al.'s six shapes, three end on a rise (rags to riches, man in a hole, Cinderella) and three on a fall (tragedy, Icarus, Oedipus); the paper reports that some arcs are downloaded more than others. — [arXiv 1606.07772](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772); [UVM appendix](https://www.uvm.edu/storylab/share/papers/reagan2016b/index.html)
- **Deferred payoffs raise the bar**: "the longer you tease something out, the bigger the payoff has to be." — [Birth.Movies.Death](https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2016/01/12/we-created-the-mystery-box.html)

### Inferences
- DreamWeaver's hardest structural problem is that length is known but the route is not. A practical scheme: at story start, record the promises (genre, tone, central question, the protagonist's want). At about 75-80% of the target length, switch the narrator into "landing" mode: introduce nothing new, converge open threads, and offer choices about *how* to resolve rather than where to go next.
- The final scene should (1) answer the central question, (2) use at least one earlier setup, (3) show what changed for the protagonist, and (4) echo an image or phrase from scene 1 (the "return"). All four fit in 3-5 sentences.
- "Surprising yet inevitable" in an interactive story mostly means the ending visibly depends on the reader's earlier choices. Referencing a specific earlier choice in the ending is the cheapest way to produce that feeling.
- For young children, rise-ending shapes (man in a hole, Cinderella) and a return to safety are the default; fall endings belong to opted-in older audiences.

### Gaps
- The phrase "surprising yet inevitable" is commonly attributed to Aristotle but is a paraphrase; I found no source for the exact wording. The related line "give the audience what it wants, but not the way it expects" (often credited to William Goldman or McKee) could not be sourced.
- I could not fetch a transcript of Sanderson's lecture (one Odyssey URL returned 404); the description above relies on the podcast show notes and search summaries.
- No research found on ending satisfaction in interactive or branching fiction (likely another researcher's scope).

## 8. Which principles are empirically backed, which are craft wisdom, which are contested or overused

### Takeaway
Very little of story craft has been tested experimentally. The honest picture is a thin layer of empirical findings (emotional arcs exist and cluster; absorption and identification are measurable; knowing the outcome does not reliably ruin enjoyment), a broad layer of convergent practitioner wisdom (causality, active wanting characters, stakes, setups paid off, endings that keep promises), and a set of popular rules that are contested or over-applied (monomyth universality, page-locked beat sheets, "show don't tell" as an absolute, conflict as mandatory, the mystery box).

### Cited Findings

**Evidence-backed (with limits)**
- Six emotional arc shapes recur in about 1,300 English-language Project Gutenberg novels, found by three independent methods; some shapes are more downloaded. Limits: sentiment of word windows is a proxy for reader emotion; corpus is old, English-language, 20k-100k words; correlational. — [arXiv 1606.07772](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772); [UVM appendix](https://www.uvm.edu/storylab/share/papers/reagan2016b/index.html)
- Narrative transportation and character identification are distinct, measurable states that correlate with each other and with liking of protagonists; much of the evidence concerns persuasion, and manipulation of transportation has often failed. — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6999344); [Green & Appel 2024](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)
- Spoilers: initial finding that knowing the ending increases enjoyment (2011); later studies conflict. Net: outcome uncertainty is not the sole driver of enjoyment. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/spoiler-alert-stories-are-not-spoiled-byspoilers.html); [Levine lab](https://wordpressua.uark.edu/whlevine/?p=122)

**Widely shared craft wisdom (independent sources converge; untested)**
- Causal chain, not sequence: Aristotle, Parker/Stone, Pixar spine, Saunders. — [SparkNotes](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/); [Cracked](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html); [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/); [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)
- Active characters who want something, try hard and sometimes fail: Pixar #1, #13, #16; Writing Excuses. — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/); [Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-10-29-why-should-my-characters-fail-spectacularly/)
- Escalation without repetition: Saunders. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/09/30/george-saunders-swim-storytelling/)
- Setups are promises; endings keep them and resolve from within: Chekhov, Aristotle, Pixar #19, Sanderson. — [Wikipedia: Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun); [Odyssey](https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=22073)
- Action/reaction rhythm: Swain. — [Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)
- Economy and simplicity: Pixar #5, #22; Parker. — [NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/pixars-rules-for-great-storytelling/); [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/south-park-creators-visit-nyu-class-lessons-storytelling-30806)

**Contested or overused**
- Hero's Journey as a universal monomyth: rejected by folklorists and classicists as cherry-picked. — [LARB](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-man-behind-the-myth-should-we-question-the-heros-journey); [Wikipedia](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Monomyth)
- Page-locked beat sheets (Save the Cat): blamed for sameness. — [Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/culturebox/2013/07/hollywood_and_blake_snyder_s_screenwriting_book_save_the_cat.html)
- Freytag's pyramid as a general plot diagram: derived from five-act tragedy. — [TECFA wiki](https://tecfalabs.unige.ch/mediawiki-narrative/index.php/Five-act_model)
- Conflict as the essence of all story: challenged by kishotenketsu, Le Guin, Salesses. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); [Le Guin](https://wiki.xxiivv.com/docs/carrier_bag.html); [Sydney Review of Books](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/review/making-craft-conscious)
- "Show, don't tell" as an absolute; the famous quotation is apocryphal. — [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2013/07/30/moon-glint/)
- Want versus need as a required schema. — [John August](https://johnaugust.com/2008/rethinking-motivation)
- Mystery box. — [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/a-mystery-box-full-of-red-matter-26806); [Birth.Movies.Death](https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2016/01/12/we-created-the-mystery-box.html)
- Chekhov's gun applied rigidly. — [Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/dont-shoot-why-i-dont-believe-in-chekhovs-gun)
- "Brain science" justifications for craft rules (Cron). — [The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/capsule-reviews-40-40525)

### Inferences

Scaling summary for DreamWeaver (my synthesis from the findings above):

| Principle | Works in a 2-5 sentence scene? | Works in a 5-scene story? | Needs 20+ scenes? | Suggested narrator instruction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Therefore / but linkage | Yes (first sentence) | Yes | No | Open each scene with a consequence of, or complication to, the last choice |
| Yes-but / no-and outcomes | Yes | Yes | No | Resolve each choice as success-with-a-wrinkle or failure-that-worsens; never plain yes |
| Scene and sequel | Yes (maps to turn loop) | Yes | No | Outcome, one beat of feeling, then a dilemma expressed as three choices |
| Stakes stated concretely | Yes | Yes | No | Say once, early, what is lost if the protagonist fails |
| One specific sensory detail | Yes | Yes | No | One non-obvious concrete detail per scene; the picture shows the rest |
| Promises, progress, payoff | Partly (progress cue) | Yes | No | Record promises at start; show progress each scene; pay off at the end |
| Setup and payoff (Chekhov) | No (spans scenes) | Yes, one pair | More pairs | Keep an open-threads list; nothing new in the final fifth |
| Emotional arc shape | No | Yes | Richer | Pick a shape at start; place the low point around two thirds |
| Story spine / Story Circle | No | Yes (compressed) | Comfortable | Use as the outline for quest stories |
| Kishotenketsu | No | Yes (4 parts) | Yes, nested | Use for gentle stories: introduce, develop, surprise, reconcile |
| Character arc (positive change) | No | Barely (one realisation) | Yes | Short stories: flat arc or single small change |
| Escalation, no repeated beats | Yes (as a check) | Yes | Critical | Each scene must change knowledge, risk or a relationship |
| Theme through choices | No | Light | Yes | Hold a thematic question; make some choices test it |
| Hero's Journey (full) / Save the Cat | No | No | Optional | Do not hard-code; offer as one template among several |

- The principles with the best combination of support and fit are the ones that operate at the level of a single turn (causal linkage, partial success or worsening failure, a real dilemma, one concrete detail) plus a light whole-story frame (promises recorded at the start, an emotional shape, a threads list, a landing phase).
- The contested items share a pattern: each is a good description of some stories that fails when enforced as a rule. For an AI narrator that suggests encoding them as selectable options or soft preferences, with the turn-level rules as defaults.
- Because so little is empirically established, DreamWeaver's own telemetry (completion rate, replays, where readers quit, which outcome types precede abandonment) would be better evidence for its audience than anything in the craft literature.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental work on fiction craft rules for interactive, AI-generated or children's stories within this scope; the empirical base cited is about static adult prose.
- Other relevant empirical lines not searched for lack of budget: Zillmann's affective disposition theory (liking characters and wanting good outcomes for them), curiosity/information-gap research (Loewenstein), Brewer and Lichtenstein's structural-affect theory (suspense, curiosity, surprise as functions of event ordering), and story-grammar research in developmental psychology (Stein and Glenn; Mandler). These would strengthen the stakes, mystery and children's-comprehension sections and are worth a follow-up.
- Several named sources in the brief (McKee, Truby, Cron, Le Guin's *Steering the Craft*) are represented only through reviews or summaries; direct quotation from them should be avoided in the final report unless verified.
