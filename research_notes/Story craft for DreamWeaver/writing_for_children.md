# Writing for children and for reading aloud (picture books, early readers, choice-based stories)

Notes compiled 2026-11-23. Each finding is tagged **[Research]** (peer-reviewed study, meta-analysis, or official evidence review) or **[Practitioner]** (author, editor, or critic opinion). Where I only reached a source through a search summary or a secondary write-up rather than reading the primary text, the finding says so. DreamWeaver-specific suggestions appear only under "Inferences".

## Picture-book craft: word economy, page turns, repetition, rhythm, rule of three, child agency, endings

### Takeaway
Practitioners converge on a small set of rules: very few words (Mem Fox's alarm bell is 500 for a whole book), text tested by ear, repetition children can join in with, a problem that the child protagonist solves through repeated attempts, and a page turn used as the unit of suspense. These are craft consensus, not experimentally tested findings.

### Cited Findings
- **[Practitioner]** Mem Fox on length: "Over 500 words means alarm bells should be ringing"; because half of a 32-page book is illustration, the text should not spell out what the pictures will show; "Your best friends are the 'cut' and 'delete' keys". — [Mem Fox, "Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox's do-list: "DO keep the text under 500 words if possible. Minimise description."; "DO write with narrative tension: solve a problem."; "DO 'show' and do not 'tell': try to reveal action and character through what the characters say and do."; "DO ensure your story makes an emotional impact: the reading should change the reader." — [Mem Fox, "Writing Do's and Don'ts"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox's don't-list: "DO NOT write down to children. Write for clever, well-adjusted, lively children."; "DO NOT write to teach. Children and publishers detest heavy morals."; "DO NOT write things like: he gasped, she spluttered, etc. Use the word 'said'."; "DO NOT write stories that end: '...and then they all woke up.'"; "DO NOT assume that plot is the most important element in a story: character comes first."; "DO NOT use alliterative names or titles, such as Izzie the Ice Cube, Kenny the Koala". — [Mem Fox, "Writing Do's and Don'ts"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox on rhythm: "Rhythm is the festering sore in imperfect drafts. It must be cured, totally."; fix awkward lines by swapping in a word with more or fewer syllables; only reading aloud shows whether the rhythm works; "The first sentence grabs and holds the listeners and readers." Rhythm, in her usage, means the placement of syllables in prose as much as verse, not rhyme. — [Mem Fox, "Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox on rhyme: "It is foolhardy to write a story with a plot... in rhyme"; rhyme suits non-narrative books. Her list repeats "DO NOT write your story in rhyme." — [Mem Fox, "Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books); [Do's and Don'ts](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox on repetition: "Children love repetition. It helps them remember the words." On stakes: "if there's no trouble, there's no story." On effect: "A child's heart should be changed between the first word and the final word." — [Mem Fox, "Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
- **[Practitioner]** Fox on page turns: make a 32-page dummy to test them, especially the last; "The page-turns are crucial to success." — [Mem Fox, "Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
- **[Practitioner]** Julia Donaldson teaches the beat before rhyme: "There is no point talking about rhyme without rhythm"; "A lot of things are written to be read aloud" (as reported in a search summary of a Tes interview; I did not read the full piece). — [Tes](https://www.tes.com/news/get-kids-reading-secret-you-know-turn-terrible-gruffalo)
- **[Practitioner]** A secondary analysis of Donaldson's books notes that The Gruffalo runs four beats per line in AABB couplets, interrupted by a repeated refrain that pauses the driving rhythm (search-summary of a writers' blog; not Donaldson's own statement). — [Clare Helen Welsh, "Six things I've learned about rhyming picture books"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24176454-six-things-i-ve-learned-about-rhyming-picture-books-by-clare-helen-welsh?tab=book)
- **[Practitioner]** Typical trade length: story picture books are "usually 32 pages long and vary between 500 to 1000 words" (publisher guidance). Other editors quoted in the same search gave 500-700 or 500-800 words. So sources differ: Fox says under 500, publishers allow up to 1,000. — [Penguin UK, "How to write a children's picture book"](https://penguin.co.uk/about/company-articles/how-to-write-a-children-s-picture-book)
- **[Practitioner]** Page turns "can be used to build suspense, upend expectations, highlight or enlarge a funny moment"; a practical method is to split a key moment across two spreads (action then consequence, or conflict then resolution) so the reader must turn to learn the outcome (search summary of editors' blog posts). — [CIEP blog, page-turns tag](https://blog.ciep.uk/tag/page-turns/); [Picture Book Den, Natascha Biebow, "Three clever ways to build up your climax"](https://picturebookden.blogspot.com/2015/07/three-clever-ways-to-build-up-your.html?m=0)
- **[Practitioner]** Rule of three as plot engine: the main character tries to solve the problem and fails, repeatedly, which builds tension and compels the page turn; Tara Lazar argues the classic three attempts is only one option and can be broken. — [Tara Lazar, "Break the rules: the rule of threes"](https://taralazar.com/2020/07/16/break-the-rules-the-rule-of-threes/)
- **[Practitioner]** Child agency: "picture books are meant to show a child that they have their own power, or agency. The protagonist is a stand in for our child reader" so the protagonist, not an adult, should resolve the problem. — [Nathan Bransford, "How picture books work: a brief primer" (2018)](https://nathanbransford.com/blog/2018/03/picture-books-work-brief-primer)
- **[Practitioner]** A common beginner mistake is that "the character solves the problem too easily"; the problem should feel huge to the child. — [John Matthew Fox](https://thejohnfox.com/?p=9566)
- **[Practitioner]** Counter-view: the convention that the protagonist "must be a child, or a bunny" is discussed and partly challenged by Maria Russo and Emily Jenkins, whose picture book is narrated by a mother. — [School Library Journal Q&A (2023)](https://www.schoollibraryjournal.com/story/qa-with-maria-russo-and-emily-jenkins-grown-ups-in-picture-books-and-the-kitten-story-a-mostly-true-tale-090623)
- **[Practitioner]** Ann Whitford Paul's "Writing Picture Books" (revised edition) has dedicated chapters on three-act structure and basic plotting, on holding the story together, on endings, and a new chapter on page turns, according to a review and the publisher listing. — [Harold Underdown review](https://underdown.org/writing-picture-books-review.htm); [Writer's Digest listing](https://writersdigest.com/wd-books/writing-picture-books)
- **[Practitioner]** Maurice Sendak's stance on honesty: he argued that children cannot be shielded ("You can't protect kids; they know everything" as quoted from a 1993 interview), and said he did not set out to write books that conquer fear but to amuse and distract; discussing Where the Wild Things Are he said Max's uncertainty about what comes next is probably scary for a child but also what a child likes most. — [Wikiquote, Maurice Sendak](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Maurice_Sendak); [NPR Fresh Air transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/114044628); [Daily Beast](https://www.thedailybeast.com/maurice-sendak-knew-enough-to-put-the-bite-back-in-childrens-stories)

### Inferences
- A DreamWeaver scene (2-5 sentences) is already close to a picture-book spread; a 12-16 scene story at "little readers" level lands in the 300-600 word range practitioners recommend. The risk is not length but description: the narrator should spend its few sentences on action, speech and feeling, and leave appearance and setting to the image.
- The choice prompt sits exactly where a page turn sits. The scene text should therefore end on an open moment (a door, a noise, a question, an attempt whose result is unknown), not on a resolved one, and the consequence should arrive in the next scene.
- Three choices per turn and three attempts per problem both map onto the rule of three; a story arc of "try, fail, try differently, fail, try again, succeed" fits a short session and lets the reader's choices be the attempts.
- For "little readers", a recurring refrain (one short line that returns every few scenes, with small variation at the climax) is the cheapest way to get repetition, join-in reading and a recognisable ending beat.
- Narrator instructions that follow directly from Fox: prefer "said"; no stated moral; never end with "it was all a dream"; the child character acts and decides, adults may be present but do not fix things; avoid cutesy alliterative names.
- Rhyme is risky for a generating model for the same reason Fox gives for humans: plot gets bent to fit the rhyme. Rhythmic prose is the safer instruction.

### Gaps
- I did not find SCBWI-authored guidance; the child-agency and word-count rules above come from individual editors and authors, not from an SCBWI document.
- I could not read Ann Whitford Paul's text directly, so her specific advice on page turns, three-act structure and endings is not quoted; only the chapter topics are confirmed.
- I found no first-hand Julia Donaldson piece setting out her method in full; the quotes are from a search summary of a Tes interview.
- None of this practitioner advice has, as far as I found, been tested experimentally (for example, whether a refrain measurably improves engagement).

## Text and illustration interplay: how words and pictures divide the work

### Takeaway
Scholars and practitioners agree the picture should not simply repeat the words: the strongest picture books let each carry different information, and meaning comes from the gap between them. For a generated image this means the prompt should carry setting, appearance, mood and visible reaction, while the text carries what cannot be seen (names, speech, thoughts, time, cause).

### Cited Findings
- **[Research/theory]** Nikolajeva and Scott describe a spectrum of word-image interaction: symmetrical (words and pictures repeat each other), complementary (each fills the other's gaps), enhancing (each enlarges the other's meaning), counterpoint (the difference is large enough that meaning lies beyond either alone), and contradiction. (Typology as summarised in secondary academic sources returned by search; category lists vary slightly between sources.) — [Nikolajeva & Scott, "The Dynamics of Picturebook Communication", Children's Literature in Education](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1023%2FA%3A1026426902123.pdf)
- **[Research/theory]** Perry Nodelman's "Words About Pictures" has chapters on "The Relationships of Pictures and Words" and "Irony in Picture Books"; a review quotes him as saying words and pictures work together by limiting each other's meanings, and a scholarly paper attributes to him the idea that a picture book tells three stories at once: the one in the words, the one in the pictures, and the one that emerges from their interaction (both via search summaries, not the book text). — [University of Georgia Press](https://ugapress.org/book/9780820355849/words-about-pictures); [JSTOR table of contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1c3sp4q)
- **[Practitioner]** Mac Barnett describes finishing a text "full of holes" that Jon Klassen partly fills with pictures, and says a good book should still leave gaps for the reader to come in and interpret (NPR conversation about "Square", via search summary). — [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/609149753)
- **[Practitioner]** Barnett has also said that in a good picture book the words and pictures should be so entangled that distinguishing them is folly, and that illustrations can carry 60-70 percent of the work (search summary of interviews). — [Art of the Picture Book interview](https://www.artofthepicturebook.com/-check-in-with/2023/11/20/an-interview-with-klassen-amp-barnett); [Book Riot interview](https://bookriot.com/the-narrative-magic-of-picture-books-an-interview-with-mac-barnett/)
- **[Practitioner]** Barnett on what to keep out of the picture: gruesome material that works in words becomes too gross when drawn and breaks the spell (the troll's fate in The Three Billy Goats Gruff). — [BookPage interview (2022)](https://bookpage.com/interviews/mac-barnett-jon-klassen-interview-the-three-billy-goats-gruff/); [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/88451-cover-reveal-the-three-billy-goats-gruff-by-mac-barnett-and-jon-klassen.html)
- **[Practitioner]** Mem Fox: "DO stay out of the illustrator's way"; do not describe in words what the picture will show. — [Mem Fox, "Writing Do's and Don'ts"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)
- **[Practitioner]** Molly Bang's "Picture This" principles, as summarised in course materials (not the book itself): smooth flat horizontal shapes feel stable and calm; verticals feel more active; diagonals imply motion or tension; pointed shapes feel threatening and curved shapes feel secure; larger objects feel stronger; red reads as danger, vitality and passion; black is associated with the unknown and its fears, white with brightness and hope; we notice contrast. — [Virginia Tech ITMA lesson on visual principles and emotions](https://www.itma.vt.edu/courses/visual/lesson_3_2.html); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/picture-this/themes)
- **[Research]** Illustrations and multimedia that are congruent with the narration help comprehension ("nonverbal information optimally attuned to the narration is beneficial for learning"); extraneous material hurts. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015), Review of Educational Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)

### Inferences
- There is a tension to manage: theory and practitioners prize counterpoint, while the multimedia research says pictures must be congruent with the narration to help young children. A reasonable reading is that complementary and enhancing images (same event, extra detail) are safe at every level, and true counterpoint or irony (picture contradicts the words) belongs to "family" and "confident readers", where the reader can resolve it.
- For "little readers" the illustration should depict the exact moment the text describes, with the protagonist visible and the key object or creature clearly shown, so the picture supports decoding and vocabulary.
- The illustration prompt should own: place, time of day, weather, what characters look like, facial expression and body language, scale relationships, and one extra detail the text does not mention (something to spot). The narrator text should own: names, dialogue, inner feeling, what just happened or is about to, sound, smell.
- Bang's principles translate into mood instructions for prompts: safe scenes use rounded shapes, horizontals, warm light, the protagonist large in frame; tense scenes use diagonals, pointed shapes, darker palette, the protagonist small. For young readers, keep scary things rounded and small relative to the hero and keep some light in every frame.
- Following Barnett, anything frightening or gross is better told in a light sentence than drawn; the prompt for such a scene should show the reaction or the aftermath, not the thing itself.

### Gaps
- I did not read Nikolajeva & Scott, Nodelman or Bang in the original; the typology and principles are taken from secondary summaries and should be checked against the books before being quoted verbatim.
- I found no research testing word-image counterpoint with AI-generated illustrations, or on how image-text mismatch from generation errors affects child comprehension.

## Developmental appropriateness by age band: language, readability, humour, fear and reassurance

### Takeaway
What frightens children shifts from what looks scary (ages roughly 3-7) to realistic and abstract dangers (later primary years), and the comfort that works shifts from physical reassurance to reasoning. Readability formulas measure only surface features and researchers warn against writing to them; humour moves from visible incongruity, to riddles and wordplay around 6-7, to irony towards 9 and up.

### Cited Findings
- **[Research]** Joanne Cantor's work (summarised in an encyclopedia entry and university and press coverage, mostly 1990s): a child's age and developmental level largely predict what will frighten them. Younger children respond to appearance: in the Incredible Hulk studies preschoolers were frightened by the character's look regardless of how it behaved, while older children took its kind or cruel behaviour into account. — [Encyclopedia.com, "Fear and the Media"](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media); [Education Week (1996)](https://www.edweek.org/education/what-scares-children/1996/04)
- **[Research]** By the later elementary years children become more sensitive to things that are dangerous but may not look scary, and to threats that are realistic rather than fantastic or impossible; they also respond to abstract threats. — [Encyclopedia.com, "Fear and the Media"](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media)
- **[Research]** Older does not mean unaffected: in one study older children were more upset than younger ones after a frightening film, and Cantor advised parents not to relax limits simply because a child is older. — [University of Wisconsin news](https://news.wisc.edu/?p=2230); [US Office of Justice Programs abstract](https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/childrens-fright-and-anxiety-responses-television-and-movies)
- **[Research]** Coping differs by age: for young children non-cognitive comfort ("a hug or a favorite toy") works better than explanation; older children benefit from logical reasoning about the unreality of a story; a calm, reassuring adult helps. — [Education Week (1996), "Scare Tactics"](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/scare-tactics/1996/02)
- **[Practitioner/theory]** Competing view: Bruno Bettelheim held that the frightening creatures and events of fairy tales help children resolve their own fears (as contrasted with Cantor in the same coverage). — [Education Week (1996), "Scare Tactics"](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/scare-tactics/1996/02)
- **[Research]** Normative fears: about 75% of typically developing children aged 4-12 report being afraid of something; fear declines in prevalence and intensity with age; fears of named imaginary creatures and of certain animals decline with age; in one study only 11% of the youngest group reported fears of bodily injury or physical danger against 53% and 55% of two older groups; social and evaluative fears rise in adolescence. (From search summaries of review articles; the exact age cut-offs of the groups were not visible.) — [Gullone, "Normative fear in childhood and adolescence", Educational and Developmental Psychologist](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/educational-and-developmental-psychologist/article/abs/normative-fear-in-childhood-and-adolescence-patterns-and-correlates/2B977E7402B5E20FF34898F1858FEDC8)
- **[Clinical guidance]** Typical fears for ages 2-4 include the dark, thunder, shadows, separation from parents and changes to routine. — [Children's Hospital of Philadelphia](https://chop.edu/news/health-tip/tackling-irrational-fears-children-and-teens)
- **[Research]** Readability formulas: Bruce, Rubin and Starr (1981) argue formulas measure surface features that do not capture what drives comprehension and ignore context; Anderson and Davison state that the properties that matter most are ones formulas cannot measure and that no formula is "a reliable guide for editing a text to reduce its difficulty"; a formula validated on one group and text type is often invalid for others. — [Bruce, Rubin & Starr, University of Illinois technical report](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/15551/bitstreams/54962/data.pdf); [Anderson & Davison, Technical Report 392](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/17909/bitstreams/64198/data.pdf)
- **[Research]** For children's web resources, lexicon-based (word familiarity) formulas predicted readability better than Flesch-Kincaid; top search results are typically written at about 12th-grade level. — [NSF-hosted paper on readability of web resources for children](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10337077)
- **[Research]** Humour development: around age 6-7 children produce more complex conceptual incongruities and wordplay such as hyperbole, puns and riddles turning on double meanings. — [Paine et al., PMC8360189](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8360189); [PMC9494906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9494906)
- **[Research]** Appreciation of irony rises with age (ratings of ironic criticism as funny were higher in older children, up to 9) while appreciation of simple riddles declines; the authors suggest children prefer humour that challenges them, and that irony is hard because it needs second-order reasoning about what the speaker believes the listener believes. — [Purser, Van Herwegen & Thomas, UCL accepted manuscript](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10087338/3/Van%20Herwegen%20Riddles%20Article%20for%20JECP%20ACCEPTED%20VERSION.pdf)
- **[Practitioner]** Voice signals age: shorter sentences, more energetic pacing and age-consistent word choice shape how old a narrator or character seems. — [Good Story Company (Mary Kole), "MG vs YA"](https://goodstorycompany.com/blog/mg-vs-ya)

### Inferences
- "Little readers" (5-7): the danger is visual. Keep monsters and villains friendly-looking or comic, never grotesque; peril should be mild, brief, and resolved within one or two scenes; reassurance should be concrete and physical (a hug, a warm light, a companion at the hero's side) rather than an explanation. Humour should be physical and visible incongruity plus simple wordplay and silly sounds.
- "Family" (around 9): readers can handle real stakes and suspense, and begin to care about realistic dangers (injury, getting lost, loss), which are therefore more potent than dragons; reasoning-based reassurance and problem-solving work. Riddles, puns, exaggeration and gentle irony fit.
- Sad or scary content is not ruled out by any source here (Sendak and Bettelheim argue for it; Cantor argues for matching it to development). The consistent requirement is a route back to safety and an adult or companion presence, which in a co-read app is the parent.
- Do not use a readability score as the target for generation. Give the narrator direct constraints (sentence count, familiar words, one idea per sentence) and treat any score only as a rough after-the-fact check.

### Gaps
- I did not find a sourced, research-based table of recommended sentence length or vocabulary size by age; the specific numbers a narrator prompt would use (for example words per sentence at 5-7) remain a design judgement.
- Cantor's findings concern television and film, mostly from the 1990s; I found no equivalent developmental study of fear responses to read-aloud or illustrated text, which is likely less intense than video.
- I did not retrieve American Academy of Pediatrics or National Literacy Trust guidance on emotional content in stories.
- The concrete-versus-abstract thinking question (Piagetian stages) is only touched on via Cantor; I did not source it independently.

## Early-reader conventions, read-aloud and dialogic reading

### Takeaway
Levelled early-reader texts are defined by short repeated sentence patterns, high-frequency words and pictures that strongly support the words, though the evidence for any one levelling system or for strict decodability is contested. Dialogic reading, where an adult prompts the child to talk about the story, has a moderate positive effect on expressive vocabulary, and the prompt types (CROWD) are a ready-made model for how choices and questions can be phrased.

### Cited Findings
- **[Practitioner/system description]** Fountas & Pinnell levelling weighs word count, number of different words, number of high-frequency words, sentence length and complexity, word repetition and illustration support. Earliest level (A): one or two lines of text per page, large spaces between words, sentence structure close to the child's own speech, highly supportive pictures. Level C: two to five lines per page, prepositional phrases and adjectives, some contractions. Levels E-F: many high-frequency words, simple and split dialogue. Around level H language stops being repetitious. Rough grade mapping: kindergarten A-D, grade 1 E-J, grade 2 K-M, grade 3 N-P. (From Wikipedia and teacher handouts, not the official descriptions.) — [Wikipedia, Fountas and Pinnell reading levels](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountas_and_Pinnell); [Verona Schools level descriptions](https://www.veronaschools.org/cms/lib/NJ01001379/Centricity/Domain/20/Fountas-Pinnell_levelsA-Z.pdf)
- **[Research]** The evidence base for that levelling system is contested: one meta-analysis of 21 studies found the gradient did not lead to effective fluency interventions, and a class-action suit was filed against the publisher in December 2024 (as reported on Wikipedia). — [Wikipedia, Fountas and Pinnell reading levels](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountas_and_Pinnell)
- **[Research]** Decodable text: evidence is mixed. Children reading decodable books are more likely to sound out words; but there is no evidence that a 90% decodable book works better than one that is 75% or 60% decodable, and definitions of "decodable" differ between studies. Timothy Shanahan argues beginners should read more than decodable texts. — [Shanahan on Literacy, via Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/should-we-teach-decodable-text-update); [Education Week (2020)](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/decodable-books-boring-useful-or-both/2020/03)
- **[Research]** A University of Newcastle systematic review judged the comparison-group evidence for decodable versus levelled books "somewhat weak". — [University of Newcastle systematic literature review](https://www.newcastle.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0020/804611/Systematic-Literature-Review-UON-Decodable-and-Levelled-Books-PDF.pdf)
- **[Research/official review]** Dialogic reading is "an interactive shared picture-book reading practice" in which adult and child switch roles so the child becomes the storyteller and the adult an active listener and questioner. It was created in the 1980s; first study Whitehurst et al. (1988), Developmental Psychology 24(4). — [What Works Clearinghouse intervention report, April 2010](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_dialogic_reading_042710.pdf)
- **[Research/official review]** CROWD prompt types: Completion (child fills in the blank at the end of a sentence), Recall (questions about a book already read), Open-ended (child tells what is happening in a picture), Wh- questions about the pictures, Distancing (relating the book to the child's own experience). PEER sequence: Prompt, Evaluate the response, Expand it, Repeat the prompt. "Children should be allowed sufficient time to respond." As the child grows familiar with a book the adult reads less and listens more. — [What Works Clearinghouse intervention report, April 2010](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_dialogic_reading_042710.pdf)
- **[Research/official review]** In that report (children aged 3-6 with language delays; two qualifying randomised trials, 52 children) WWC rated dialogic reading as having "potentially positive effects" on communication and language, average improvement index +11 percentile points (range -2 to +45), with the extent of evidence "small". — [What Works Clearinghouse intervention report, April 2010](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_dialogic_reading_042710.pdf)
- **[Research]** Mol, Bus, de Jong & Smeets (2008) meta-analysis of 16 studies of dialogic parent-child reading: moderate effect on expressive vocabulary (reported as 0.59 in a secondary evidence review) and a small effect on receptive vocabulary; benefits were smaller for older (4-5-year-old) children and those most at risk of school failure (per abstract fragments and later reviews; I could not read the subgroup figures). — [Leiden University repository, "To read or not to read"](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2899524/view); [University of Western Ontario critical review](https://www.uwo.ca/fhs/csd/ebp/reviews/2012-13/Atkinson_A.pdf)
- **[Research]** With tablet books, parents used less dialogic reading: in a lab study of 37 parent-toddler pairs, print books produced more parent and child talk about the story and more collaboration than either enhanced or basic e-books, suggesting the tablet itself, not only the enhancements, interfered. — [Munzer et al. (2019), Pediatrics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6564071); [AAP news release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/pediatrics2/2019/parents-and-children-interact-with-print-and-electronic-books-differrently/)
- **[Practitioner]** Mem Fox on reading aloud: the rhythm of the text "is the element that will or will not bring the reader back". — [Mem Fox, "Writing Do's and Don'ts"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)

### Inferences
- "Little readers" text should borrow levelled-reader conventions without claiming to be decodable: short sentences in natural spoken order, common words, one new or interesting word per scene at most (and shown in the picture), a repeating sentence frame across scenes, dialogue with plain "said", and a picture that shows what the sentence says.
- A choice prompt is functionally a dialogic prompt. Phrasing choices as open or wh- questions about the story ("What should Mila do about the stuck gate?") and occasionally as a completion of the refrain keeps the interaction on the story. A "distancing" option (connecting to the child's own life) could be offered to the co-reading adult as an optional talk prompt, not as a plot choice.
- The PEER sequence suggests the next scene should acknowledge and expand the child's choice explicitly ("You chose to knock. Knock, knock, knock!"), which also gives the repetition young readers like.
- Because the screen tends to suppress adult talk (Munzer), the "family" mode should deliberately leave room for it: a pause before choices, and a visible prompt aimed at the adult.
- The browser read-aloud voice makes rhythm a functional requirement: sentences should be speakable in one breath, with no parentheses, abbreviations or symbols.

### Gaps
- The WWC report I could read is the children-with-disabilities version; the general-population dialogic-reading report and its ratings were not retrieved.
- Dialogic reading research is concentrated on ages 2-6; I found little on its effect for 7-9-year-olds or on read-aloud benefits for older children (the Scholastic Kids & Family Reading Report was not retrieved).
- I found no study that tests in-story plot choices (as opposed to adult questions) as a comprehension aid.
- No sourced list of high-frequency words (Dolch, Fry) was retrieved.

## Interactive and choice-based stories, digital picture books and interactivity

### Takeaway
The best-established finding is that multimedia aligned with the story helps young children's comprehension while hotspots, games and embedded dictionaries distract from it, especially for disadvantaged children; an adult reading alongside still beats enhancements. Choice-based fiction design suggests a branch-and-rejoin structure with friendly rather than deadly side paths.

### Cited Findings
- **[Research]** Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015), meta-analysis of 43 studies, 2,147 children: technology-enhanced stories gave a small advantage over traditional presentation for story comprehension (g+ = 0.17) and expressive vocabulary (g+ = 0.20); no significant effect on receptive vocabulary, code-related skills or engagement. — [Review of Educational Research 85(4), via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- **[Research]** In the same meta-analysis, multimedia-only stories (animation, music, sound effects congruent with the story) gave comprehension g+ = 0.39 and expressive vocabulary g+ = 0.24; stories with interactive plus multimedia features gave comprehension g+ = -0.14 (not significant) and interactive-only -0.03 (not significant). Whether the interactive features were relevant to the story did not significantly moderate the effect (p = .48). "Interactive elements seem to distract from understanding the story and result in cognitive overload in the child." — [Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- **[Research]** For disadvantaged children, multimedia-only stories gave comprehension g+ = 0.66; "Multimedia elements were especially helpful and interactive features were especially distracting for these children." The authors recommend stories "without interactive features that might distract children from the story". — [Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- **[Research]** Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus (2021), meta-analysis of 39 studies, 1,812 children aged about 1-8: when digital and paper versions differed only by digitisation, digital gave lower comprehension; with story-congruent enhancements digital outperformed paper; adult mediation of print reading was more effective than enhancements in digital books read independently; an embedded dictionary had no or a negative effect on story comprehension but helped vocabulary. — [Review of Educational Research 91(4), Open University repository](https://oro.open.ac.uk/75652); [Education Week (2021)](https://edweek.org/teaching-learning/reading-on-screen-vs-print-new-analysis-thickens-the-plot-on-promoting-comprehension/2021/07)
- **[Research]** Press coverage of the same study reports that audio narration alone did little, and that Adriana Bus suggested disadvantaged children are more likely to be distracted by interactive features and devices, a point the authors said needs more research. — [Education Week (2021)](https://edweek.org/teaching-learning/reading-on-screen-vs-print-new-analysis-thickens-the-plot-on-promoting-comprehension/2021/07)
- **[Research/theory]** Natalia Kucirkova's work on personalised digital books treats agency as double-edged: teachers and industry professionals associated digital personalisation with potential both to enhance and to jeopardise children's and adults' agency; Kucirkova and Mackey (2020) argue AI-personalised texts can reduce a child's agency in forming a sense of identity. (Abstract-level, via search.) — [Kucirkova & Flewitt, London Review of Education](https://journals.uclpress.co.uk/lre/article/id/352); [UCL personalised stories project](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/departments-and-centres/learning-and-leadership/research/personalised-stories)
- **[History]** Choose Your Own Adventure began with Edward Packard's bedtime stories: when he could not decide how a story should go he asked his children, and played out both options when they disagreed. Sugarcane Island was written in 1969 and published in 1976; Bantam published almost 300 titles from 1979 to 1998. Packard describes the format as one where the reader is the star and makes choices leading to multiple plots and endings. Later books experimented with loops and trick endings. — [Edward Packard, "A brief history of Choose Your Own Adventure"](https://edwardpackard.com/?p=11); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choose_Your_Own_Adventure); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/literature/books/brief-history-choose-your-own-adventure)
- **[Practitioner]** Sam Kabo Ashwell's structural patterns for choice-based works: the "time cave" (constant branching, no rejoining) gives short, replayable stories but the reader has "velocity but little grasp" and misses most content; the "gauntlet" (a mostly linear thread with side branches that die, backtrack or rejoin) is "perhaps the easiest structure to author", most readers see most of the content, but deadly versions feel punishing and friendly gauntlets are "vastly more common in recent years"; "branch and bottleneck" (branches rejoin at common events, with remembered state) lets the reader build a distinctive story while the plot stays manageable but needs length "to accumulate change before producing results"; "loop and grow" returns to the same point with new options, keeping the world regular. — [Sam Kabo Ashwell, "Standard patterns in choice-based games" (2015)](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)
- **[Practitioner]** A meaningful choice is one the player is aware of, that has consequences, that the work reminds them of later, and that cannot be undone (Brice Morrison's framework, via a study guide); a separate analysis notes that well-regarded choice games branch less than they appear to. — [Study of Interactive Fiction notes](https://rachels-organization-4.gitbook.io/study-of-interactive-fiction/videogame-book-club/narrative-types-and-choices/choice-of-robots); [Game Developer, "Illusion of choice is better than choice"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/illusion-of-choice-is-better-than-choice-choices-and-illusions-as-narrative-mechanics)

### Inferences
- DreamWeaver's one interaction per scene, placed after the text and directly about the plot, is structurally different from the tap-anywhere hotspots and mini-games in the studies, so the negative findings do not transfer cleanly. But Takacs et al. found that even story-relevant interactivity did not reliably help, so the cautious design is: no interaction during narration, choices only at the scene boundary, no decorative animation or sound unrelated to the scene, no inline dictionary pop-ups for "little readers".
- A fixed illustration that matches the narration is the congruent "nonverbal scaffolding" the research favours; image-text mismatch from generation is the main thing to prevent.
- Because an adult co-reader outperforms enhancements, the "family" level is the configuration best supported by evidence, and the app should encourage it for the youngest readers rather than position read-aloud audio as a substitute for a person.
- Structure: for young children use a friendly gauntlet or branch-and-bottleneck (choices change how the hero gets there and are echoed later, but the story reconverges and always reaches a safe ending). Avoid sudden-death or "wrong choice" endings for "little readers"; a poor choice should lead to a funny setback and another try, which also supplies the rule-of-three attempts.
- Following the meaningful-choice criteria, the next scene should name the choice the reader made, and a later scene should call back to an earlier one. Three options should be clearly different actions with visible, concrete outcomes, and for the youngest level none should be a trap.
- Kucirkova's caution suggests letting the child shape the hero and the direction, rather than the system silently personalising the story to the child.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental research on branching or choice-based narratives for children specifically (comprehension, engagement or reading motivation); the design guidance here comes from game-writing practitioners addressing general audiences.
- I did not find analysis of Choose Your Own Adventure's ending design (ratio of good to bad endings, death endings) from a reliable source.
- No research was found on AI-generated or AI-narrated storybooks for children as of this search.
- Kucirkova's "Children's agency by design" (2019) was identified by citation only; its design parameters were not retrieved.

## Representation and inclusivity

### Takeaway
The standard framing is Rudine Sims Bishop's "mirrors, windows and sliding glass doors": children need books that reflect themselves and books that open onto other lives. I found the framing but little concrete, citable guidance on avoiding stereotypes in generated characters.

### Cited Findings
- **[Scholarship]** Rudine Sims Bishop (1990): "Books are sometimes windows, offering views of worlds that may be real or imagined, familiar or strange... When lighting conditions are just right, however, a window can also be a mirror"; the windows "are also sliding glass doors, and readers have only to walk through in imagination to become part of whatever world has been created". Published in Perspectives: Choosing and Using Books for the Classroom, 6(3). — [Facing History & Ourselves reprint](https://www.facinghistory.org/resource-library/mirrors-windows-sliding-glass-doors); [Wikipedia, Rudine Sims Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudine_Sims_Bishop)
- **[Research/practitioner]** The UK Centre for Literacy in Primary Education quantifies the under-representation of characters of colour in UK children's literature through its Reflecting Realities research and has published guidance on curating inclusive book stock (book description only; the guidance itself was not retrieved). — [CLPE, "Representation in Children's Literature" (Sage/Corwin, 2024)](https://corwin.com/books/reflecting-realities-280965)
- **[Research]** Readability and text difficulty also depend on the reader's dialect and cultural background, which formulas ignore. — [Bruce, Rubin & Starr, University of Illinois](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/15551/bitstreams/54962/data.pdf)

### Inferences
- For generated stories: vary who the hero is by default (appearance, family shape, ability, setting) instead of defaulting to one type; let any child be the one who solves the problem; keep identity incidental to the plot unless the reader chooses otherwise; describe characters in the illustration prompt by specific, neutral visual traits instead of group labels that image models tend to caricature; avoid coding villains through appearance, accent or disability.
- Mem Fox's advice against cute alliterative animal names and against writing down to children is compatible with this: specific, individual characters resist stereotype better than types.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve CLPE's Reflecting Realities figures or its specific criteria for quality representation, nor guidance from We Need Diverse Books or similar bodies.
- I found no source addressing stereotype risks in AI-generated children's characters or illustrations; the inferences above are design reasoning, not sourced guidance.

## How the principles change for older children, teens and adults

### Takeaway
Publishing convention separates middle grade (readers about 8-12) from young adult (13-18) mainly by content limits, protagonist age and voice; research on fear and humour indicates that older readers want realism, moral complexity in characters, irony and challenge. Interactivity findings from young-child studies do not extend to teens and adults.

### Cited Findings
- **[Practitioner]** Middle grade is generally pitched at readers 8-12 with protagonists about 10-13; young adult at 13-18 with protagonists 14-18; young readers like to read about characters older than themselves. — [Writer's Digest, "The key differences between middle grade vs young adult"](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/the-key-differences-between-middle-grade-vs-young-adult); [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/ya-vs-mg-fiction/)
- **[Practitioner]** Content: middle grade is typically without profanity or sexual content; young adult may include swearing, violence and more mature material; the difference between the two is chiefly subject matter. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/ya-vs-mg-fiction/); [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/the-key-differences-between-middle-grade-vs-young-adult)
- **[Practitioner]** Voice: sentence length, energy and age-consistent word choice are what make a narrative read as younger or older. — [Good Story Company, "MG vs YA"](https://goodstorycompany.com/blog/mg-vs-ya)
- **[Research]** Older children judge a character by behaviour and motive rather than appearance, respond to realistic and abstract threats, can be more upset than younger children by frightening material, and are helped by reasoning. — [Encyclopedia.com, "Fear and the Media"](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/fear-and-media); [University of Wisconsin news](https://news.wisc.edu/?p=2230)
- **[Research]** Social and evaluative fears rise in adolescence. — [Gullone, Educational and Developmental Psychologist](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/educational-and-developmental-psychologist/article/abs/normative-fear-in-childhood-and-adolescence-patterns-and-correlates/2B977E7402B5E20FF34898F1858FEDC8)
- **[Research]** Older children prefer humour that is not obvious; irony appreciation increases with age as riddle appreciation falls. — [Purser, Van Herwegen & Thomas, UCL](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10087338/3/Van%20Herwegen%20Riddles%20Article%20for%20JECP%20ACCEPTED%20VERSION.pdf)
- **[Practitioner]** For longer choice-based works, tracking state so that earlier choices shape character and are reflected later (branch and bottleneck) is what lets a reader "construct a distinctive story"; purely branching structures leave the reader with freedom but little grasp. — [Sam Kabo Ashwell (2015)](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)
- **[Practitioner]** Picture-book makers themselves value gaps the reader must interpret, which is an older-reader skill. — [NPR, Mac Barnett and Jon Klassen](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/609149753)

### Inferences
- "Confident readers": drop the refrain and overt repetition; allow longer and more varied sentences, subtext, unreliable or ironic narration, morally mixed antagonists judged by motive, and stakes that are realistic and social (reputation, loyalty, loss) as well as physical. Choices can be dilemmas with trade-offs and lasting consequences, and failure endings become acceptable.
- The illustration can move from showing the event to counterpoint: mood, symbol, or a detail that contradicts or complicates the text.
- What stays constant across levels: economy (2-5 sentences still demands cutting description), speakable rhythm for read-aloud, ending each scene on an open beat, and a protagonist who drives events.
- "Family" level sits at the middle-grade boundary: no profanity or mature content, a protagonist slightly older than nine, real but resolvable stakes, wordplay and light irony, and text an adult enjoys reading aloud (Fox: content should interest both children and adults).

### Gaps
- I found no research on choice-based or illustrated short fiction for teens and adults; the older-reader guidance rests on publishing convention and extrapolation from fear and humour studies.
- No sourced guidance was found on how the text-illustration relationship should change for adult readers in a picture-book format (graphic-novel and illustrated-fiction scholarship was not searched).
