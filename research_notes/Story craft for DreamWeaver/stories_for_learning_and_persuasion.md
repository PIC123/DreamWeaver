# Stories for learning and persuasion: what the evidence shows (notes for DreamWeaver)

Research date: 2026-11-23. Conventions: effect sizes are quoted as reported (r, d, g). "Second-hand" means the number was taken from a later paper or summary, not the original, and should be checked before it is printed. "Inferences" are my design-rule translations, not findings.

Rough reading guide for effect sizes: r = .10 / d = .20 small; r = .30 / d = .50 medium; d = .80 large.

---

## 1. Narrative transportation, identification, E-ELM and the persuasion meta-analyses: what increases transportation/identification, and how large are the effects?

### Takeaway
Stories do shift beliefs, attitudes, intentions and behaviour, but the average effect is small (r about .17 to .23; narrative-versus-non-narrative advantage about d = .13 to .17), it persists for months at about the same small size, and it runs through absorption in the story and connection with characters rather than through argument quality. The story features that reliably raise transportation are identifiable characters, an imaginable plot and believability (verisimilitude); perspective and psychological (not demographic) similarity drive identification.

### Cited Findings
- **Braddock and Dillard (2016, Communication Monographs)**, meta-analysis of narrative exposure versus control: narrative-consistent beliefs r = .17 (k = 37, N = 7,376), attitudes r = .19 (k = 40, N = 7,132), intentions r = .17 (k = 28, N = 5,211), behaviours r = .23 (k = 5, N = 978). Medium of presentation and research design did not moderate; fictionality gave mixed results; unidentified moderators remain. — [Braddock & Dillard 2016, DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555); summary also in [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/society/can-stories-change-us)
- Note the behaviour estimate rests on only 5 studies, and the meta-analysis covers immediate effects only. — [Braddock & Dillard 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555); limitation noted in [Oschatz & Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)
- **Shen, Sheer and Li (2015, Journal of Advertising)**, health narratives versus non-narrative messages: narratives are more persuasive by d = .13 (r = .063, p < .01) immediately after exposure (figure as restated by Oschatz and Marker). Audio and video narratives produced significant effects; print-based narratives did not. Effects were stronger for detection and prevention behaviours. — [Oschatz & Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf); [Shen, Sheer & Li 2015 record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/)
- **Oschatz and Marker (2020, Journal of Communication)**, 14 studies, 51 immediate and 66 delayed effect sizes: narrative beats non-narrative by d = .14 (95% CI .048 to .239) immediately and d = .16 at delay; at delay, attitudes d = .19, intentions d = .13, behaviours d = .15 (CI .013 to .278); no reliable advantage for beliefs. Immediate and delayed effects were statistically indistinguishable over 1 to 180 days (mean 62 days): "the greater persuasive power is persistent up to six months after reception". Transportation mediated the immediate advantage but not the delayed one. Almost all studies were health topics with adults. At delay, the advantage held for non-fictional narratives (d = .11) but was not reliable for fictional ones (d = .08). — [Oschatz & Marker 2020 (PDF)](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)
- **van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti and Wetzels (2014, Journal of Consumer Research)**, 132 effect sizes from 76 articles (extended transportation-imagery model). Story-side antecedents that significantly raise transportation: identifiable characters, an imaginable plot, and verisimilitude. Receiver-side antecedents: transportability (trait), attention, familiarity with the story topic, education, and sex. Consequences of transportation: stronger affective responses, more narrative thoughts, fewer critical thoughts, and story-consistent beliefs, attitudes and intentions. — [van Laer et al. 2014 record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/the-extended-transportation-imagery-model-a-meta-analysis-of-the-/); [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/06/transportation-into-narrative-worlds.html)
- **Tukachinsky and Tokunaga (2013)** meta-analysed engagement with entertainment narratives; a later article reports their transportation to belief/attitude association as r = .32 (second-hand). The literature distinguishes involvement with the story (transportation) from involvement with characters (identification, parasocial interaction). — [International Journal of Communication 2021 article](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/17446/3612)
- **Green and Brock (2000, JPSP 79(5), 701 to 721)** introduced transportation as the mechanism: absorption in a story leads to story-consistent beliefs; their scale underlies most later work. — [Transportation theory overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology)); [Oschatz & Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)
- **Identification is manipulable through perspective.** De Graaf, Hoeken, Sanders and Beentjes (2012, Communication Research): telling the same story from one character's perspective raised identification with that character, and identification mediated the shift in attitudes toward that character's position. — [de Graaf et al. 2012](https://dare.uva.nl/id/0120bd02-1b30-4c0b-9a47-1d8a832dccd9)
- **Similarity: psychological beats demographic.** Huang, Fung and Sun (2024, Current Psychology; 39 studies, k = 50, N = 11,077): manipulating psychological similarity (attitudes, situation, goals) produced larger effects than objective/demographic similarity; the demographic-similarity-to-identification estimate rested on only six effects from four studies. A systematic review by de Graaf and colleagues found similarity manipulations "hardly ever" affected identification, transportation or persuasion. — [Huang, Fung & Sun 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-023-04842-4)
- **Why narrative persuades (E-ELM and successors).** Moyer-Gusé and Nabi (2010, Human Communication Research) found a dramatic narrative reduced reactance by fostering parasocial interaction and lowering perceived persuasive intent, and identification reduced counter-arguing; this extends Slater and Rouner's E-ELM claim that absorption suppresses counter-arguing. — [summary in Cambridge core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/07CABFC5ABA0D3A6A369FCDDB21F8F76/core-reader)
- **Ratcliff and Sun (2020)** meta-analysis: relative to non-narrative messages, narratives are more likely to reduce resistance to persuasion, and resistance is predicted by level of engagement with the narrative. — [Ratcliff & Sun 2020 record](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/overcoming-resistance-through-narratives-findings-from-a-meta-ana/)

### Inferences
- Expect a small nudge per story, not conversion. A single short story is a d of roughly .15 intervention; cumulative exposure and repetition across stories is where meaningful change would have to come from (cumulative effects are not directly tested in these meta-analyses).
- Design levers with evidence behind them: (a) one clearly drawn protagonist with a goal the reader shares; (b) plot that is easy to picture (concrete, visualisable scenes: illustrations help here); (c) internal consistency and believability even in fantasy worlds; (d) tell the scene from the perspective of the character whose view you want adopted; (e) match the protagonist's situation, worries and goals to the reader rather than their age/gender/appearance.
- Interactivity (a choice each turn) plausibly raises attention, which van Laer lists as an antecedent of transportation, but choice prompts are also interruptions. I found no meta-analytic estimate for interactive fiction specifically.
- Fictional stories show weaker long-run persuasive advantage than non-fictional ones in the one long-term meta-analysis; do not over-promise durable attitude change from fantasy stories.

### Gaps
- Exact r values for each van Laer antecedent and consequence were not retrievable (open-access copies blocked automated access). The published tables are at [doi 10.1086/673383](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/673383).
- Jonathan Cohen's (2001) definition of identification and Slater and Rouner's (2002) E-ELM paper were not read directly; they are represented here through later papers that build on them.
- Almost all meta-analytic evidence is from adults and health topics. I found no meta-analysis of narrative persuasion in children.
- Effect of reader choice/agency on transportation and persuasion: no reliable quantitative synthesis found.

## 2. Why explicit morals and heavy-handed messages fail or backfire; children's understanding of story morals

### Takeaway
Two separate problems: (1) for older children and adults, visible persuasive intent triggers reactance and counter-arguing, which narrative otherwise suppresses; (2) for young children the problem is the opposite: they often do not extract the abstract moral at all, anchor on surface details, and transfer less from anthropomorphic or fantastical characters. Asking the child to explain why events happened works better than stating the moral.

### Cited Findings
- Narrative reduces reactance by lowering perceived persuasive intent; identification reduces counter-arguing (Moyer-Gusé and Nabi 2010). The corollary is that making intent overt removes the mechanism. — [Cambridge core reader summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/07CABFC5ABA0D3A6A369FCDDB21F8F76/core-reader)
- Meta-analytically, narratives reduce resistance relative to non-narratives, and the reduction tracks engagement (Ratcliff and Sun 2020). — [Ratcliff & Sun 2020](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/overcoming-resistance-through-narratives-findings-from-a-meta-ana/)
- Transported readers produce fewer critical thoughts (van Laer et al. 2014). — [van Laer et al. 2014 record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/the-extended-transportation-imagery-model-a-meta-analysis-of-the-/)
- **Children often miss the moral.** Narvaez, Gleason, Mitchell and Bentley (1999, Journal of Educational Psychology 91(3), 477 to 487) asked 3rd and 5th graders to identify the moral of a story and pick a new story with the same theme. As summarised by Walker and Lombrozo, children did not reliably extract the theme until about 4th grade (about age 10), 3rd graders performed worst, and errors reflected distraction by superficial details. — [Walker & Lombrozo 2017 (author copy)](https://cognition.princeton.edu/document/131); [Narvaez et al. 1999 record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/measuring-moral-cognition-in-children-moral-theme-comprehension-a-2/)
- **Explaining beats being told.** Walker and Lombrozo (2017, Cognition 167, 266 to 281): 5 and 6 year olds (48 in Study 1) heard an illustrated storybook with a moral. Children prompted to explain story events ("why did...?") were better at overriding salient surface features and identifying/generalising the moral than children asked merely to report events. The authors report that "in some cases, generating an explanation is more effective than being explicitly told the moral of the story". — [Walker & Lombrozo 2017](https://cognition.princeton.edu/publications/explaining-moral-story); [EdWeek coverage](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/one-key-question-may-help-children-get-deeper-meaning-from-stories/2017/08)
- **Human versus anthropomorphic characters.** Larsen, Lee and Ganea (2017 online / 2018 print, Developmental Science): 4 to 6 year olds had a sticker-sharing opportunity before and after hearing one of three books: a sharing story with human characters, the same story with anthropomorphic animals, or a control book about seeds. Sharing rose after the human book and fell after the animal book and the control. Most children did not see the animal characters as human-like; those who did attribute human traits to them shared more after the animal book. Children showed no preference between the human and animal versions. — [University of Toronto release](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/choosing-dora-over-franklin-u-t-study-says-children-learn-more-human-characters-not-animals); [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- A conceptual replication with a different sample and extended age range exists (accepted manuscript, Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, "The animals in moral tales"); it frames the original finding as moderated by character realism. I did not retrieve its results. — [Lancaster University manuscript](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/165266/1/The_Animals_in_Moral_Tales_JECP_accepted_manuscript.pdf)
- Review-level interpretation: lack of identification with non-human characters "may hinder transfer of the moral"; the most supportive thing adults can do is talk with children during reading, discuss underlying structure, and prompt explanation. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- A secondary summary of developmental work states that stories change children's behaviour mainly when they are directly applicable to a context the child is dealing with, with fanciful tales having little effect. — [Psychology Today (secondary)](https://psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/moral-boundaries/202010/do-children-learn-moral-lessons-storybooks)

### Inferences
- "Don't preach" has two different meanings by age. For adults and older children: keep persuasive intent low, let consequences in the plot carry the message, avoid a narrator verdict. For under-10s: do not assume the theme will be inferred; the fix is not a stated moral but an in-story prompt to explain ("Why do you think Mira gave the lantern back?") and a second, different-surface situation where the same idea applies.
- A natural DreamWeaver mechanic: a character asks the reader a why-question as part of the dialogue, and the next choice requires applying the same principle in a new setting. This is explanation plus near transfer, with no narrator moralising.
- For prosocial or behavioural lessons aimed at 4 to 6 year olds, prefer human (or clearly human-like, human-situated) protagonists; talking animals are fine for entertainment but the evidence suggests weaker behavioural transfer. Treat this as one study plus a conceptual replication, not settled.
- Children did not prefer animal books over human books in Larsen et al., so there is little engagement cost to choosing human characters for lesson stories.

### Gaps
- Exact proportions of children who extracted the theme in Narvaez et al. (1999) were not retrievable (PDF blocked).
- No direct experimental test found of "stated moral at the end" versus "no stated moral" on reactance in children; the reactance evidence is adult.
- Results of the Larsen et al. conceptual replication were not read.

## 3. Emotional engagement: feeling for characters; suspense, curiosity, surprise; fiction and empathy (with replication concerns)

### Takeaway
Emotional pull is largely a matter of information ordering (what the reader knows and when) plus an identifiable character with something at stake; curiosity states also boost memory, including for incidental material. The headline claim that reading (literary) fiction improves theory of mind is weak: the single-passage effect failed large replications and the best experimental estimates are tiny (g about .14 to .16), with a null in a recent RCT.

### Cited Findings
- **Structural affect theory (Brewer and Lichtenstein 1981/1982).** The order in which events are narrated evokes different affects: withholding the outcome of an initiating event produces suspense; presenting an outcome before the events that explain it produces curiosity; an unexpected event (critical information withheld without the reader knowing) produces surprise. Their experiments varied discourse structure (standard suspense, foreshadowing, misarranged, no-resolution, surprise versions) and collected page-by-page affect ratings and end-of-story liking; stories that create and then resolve these affects are liked more. — [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981 summary (Univ. of Alberta)](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm); [later discussion (Univ. of Amsterdam)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2559022/169246_05_1_.pdf)
- A 2000 Poetics study ("Suspense, curiosity, and surprise: how discourse structure influences the affective and cognitive processing of a story", attributed to Hoeken and van Vliet) found linear telling evoked the most suspense and reversal-type structures the most curiosity (attribution and details from secondary records; unverified against the full text). — [catalogue record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_85525221)
- **Information-gap theory (Loewenstein 1994, Psychological Bulletin).** Curiosity arises from a perceived gap between what one knows and what one wants to know; it is strongest when the gap is small and feels closable, and weak when the person knows too little to notice a gap; curiosity therefore peaks at intermediate knowledge. — [Golman & Loewenstein on curiosity](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf); [review of curiosity and learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmc6891259)
- **Curiosity improves memory, including for incidental material.** Reviews of Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014, Neuron) report that people remember high-curiosity answers better and that even unrelated information presented during a high-curiosity state is better remembered later. Evidence is from adults in laboratory trivia paradigms. — [review, PMC6891259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmc6891259)
- Identifiable characters, imaginable plot and verisimilitude raise transportation, and transportation raises affective responses (van Laer et al. 2014). — [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/06/transportation-into-narrative-worlds.html)
- **Fiction and empathy: contested.** Kidd and Castano (2013, Science) reported that reading short literary fiction passages immediately improved Reading the Mind in the Eyes (RMET) scores versus popular fiction or non-fiction. Panero et al. (2016), three labs, N = 792: "no significant advantage in RMET scores for literary fiction compared to any of the other conditions". — [Panero et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27642659/)
- A direct replication of Kidd and Castano Experiment 5 (van Kuijk et al. 2018) was largely consistent with the original, but its small meta-analysis found the effect of reading literary fiction on theory of mind "small and non-significant" with considerable heterogeneity. — [van Kuijk et al. 2018](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/the-effect-of-reading-a-short-passage-of-literary-fiction-on-theo/)
- Dodell-Feder and Tamir (2018, JEP: General; 14 experiments, 53 effect sizes): fiction reading produces a small improvement in social cognition, g = .15 to .16. — [Dodell-Feder & Tamir 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29481102/)
- Wimmer et al. (2024, JEP: General), two preregistered multilevel meta-analyses: experimental effect g = 0.14, significant only for empathy and mentalising; correlational association of lifetime print-fiction exposure r = .16. — [Wimmer et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38602788/)
- A 2023 randomised study by Dodell-Feder and colleagues found fiction readers did not outperform non-fiction readers or non-readers on any social outcome. — [Dodell-Feder et al. 2023](https://labsites.rochester.edu/scplab/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/The_impact_of_fiction_reading_.pdf)
- Correlational meta-analysis (Mumper and Gerrig 2017): lifetime fiction reading has small-to-medium associations with empathy and theory of mind; causality is not established. — [Mumper & Gerrig 2017 record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/leisure-reading-and-social-cognition-a-meta-analysis/)

### Inferences
- Each scene can be engineered around one of three affect structures: suspense (reader knows the danger, outcome delayed past the choice point), curiosity (show an odd outcome first, let the reader's choices uncover the cause), surprise (a reveal that recontextualises). Ending a scene on an open question just before the choice is the structural-affect way to make the choice matter.
- Curiosity is the affect best aligned with teaching: open a small, closable gap about the target concept ("the message arrived in pieces, out of order; how did the tower put it back together?"), let the reader commit to a guess via the choice, then resolve it. The gap must be pitched at intermediate knowledge: give enough set-up that the reader can see what they do not know.
- Because incidental material encountered during curiosity is also remembered better (adult lab data), put the key idea at or right after the moment of resolution, not in a calm aside.
- Do not market DreamWeaver as "builds empathy" on the strength of Kidd and Castano; the defensible statement is "small, inconsistent effects of fiction on social cognition".

### Gaps
- Mar and Oatley's simulation account of fiction was not read directly; represented via the meta-analyses that test it.
- Camerer et al. (2018) large-scale replication project result for Kidd and Castano was not confirmed in the sources retrieved.
- Evidence on curiosity-driven memory in children, and in story (rather than trivia) contexts, was not found.
- Loewenstein (1994) primary text was not read; description is from later papers by Loewenstein and co-authors and reviews.

## 4. Learning through narrative: narrative versus expository text, narrative-centred learning environments, anchored instruction, and limits

### Takeaway
Narrative text is understood and remembered better than expository text (g = .55), and the advantage is at least as large for children; but this is about text genre, not proof that wrapping any lesson in a story or game helps. In game studies, more story is associated with smaller, not larger, learning gains, and a head-to-head test found narrative discovery games taught less than a plain slideshow. Narrative helps when the to-be-learned content is the causal backbone of the story.

### Cited Findings
- **Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review 28, 732 to 749)**: 37 articles, 150 effect sizes, 78 samples, 33,078 participants. Narrative texts beat expository texts on memory and comprehension, g = .55 (95% CI .31 to .79). Memory g = .72; comprehension g = .48 (difference p = .09). Non-adults g = .67 versus adults g = .44 (difference not significant). Listening g = .61 versus reading g = .54. Delayed tests g = .53 (only 4 studies). — [Mar et al. 2021 (open access)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)
- Caveats from the same paper: heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 98%) and unexplained by moderators; Egger's test indicated possible publication bias, specifically for memory outcomes; only 28% of effect sizes came from studies that controlled content across the two genres, though the content-controlled estimate was similar (g = .57, CI -.13 to 1.27, i.e. imprecise). — [Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)
- For inferential comprehension specifically, Clinton and colleagues (2020) found a smaller narrative advantage, g = .36. — [Clinton et al. 2020 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618145.pdf)
- For young children's physics learning, genre did not matter in one study: children learned and transferred gravity concepts equally well from informational and narrative picture books (Venkadasalam and Ganea 2017, as reviewed). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- **Games meta-analysis, Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth (2016, Review of Educational Research)**: digital games beat non-game instruction, g = 0.33 (CI 0.19 to 0.48). On story: games with no story (g = 0.44) or thin story (g = 0.47) outperformed games with medium story depth (g = -0.03); thick story g = 0.36 but only five studies. Games with story irrelevant to the content had larger effects (g = 0.63) than those with relevant story (g = 0.17), a difference that vanished after controls. More contextualisation overall was associated with smaller effects (b = -0.07, p = .01). Schematic visuals (g = 0.48) outperformed realistic visuals (g = -0.01). Enhanced scaffolding in value-added comparisons: g = 0.41. Teacher-provided scaffolding (g = 0.58) beat simple success/fail/points feedback (g = 0.26). The authors treat moderator findings as tentative (small cells, confounding). — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- **Wouters et al. (2013, Journal of Educational Psychology)** serious-games meta-analysis: the narrative moderator was not significant, with a trend suggesting games without narrative might be more effective. — [Wouters et al. 2013 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1349790206); summary in [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- **Crystal Island (Lester and colleagues, NC State).** A narrative-centred science mystery for eighth-grade microbiology. In a study of 153 eighth graders, students who were more engaged with the environment achieved better learning outcomes and problem solving (Rowe, Shores, Mott and Lester 2011); the authors themselves flag the concern that narrative and gameplay elements may not contribute to learning. — [Rowe et al. 2011](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/rowe2011integrating.pdf)
- **Head-to-head test.** Adams, Mayer, MacNamara, Koenig and Wainess (2012, Journal of Educational Psychology 104, 235 to 249): college students who played Crystal Island learned less (retention and transfer) and rated the lesson harder than students who saw a matched slideshow with no narrative or hands-on activity; results were similar for a second narrative game, Cache 17. — [ATD summary of Adams et al. 2012](https://www.td.org/insights/why-games-dont-teach); [abstract record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2012039745)
- **Anchored instruction (Jasper Woodbury, Cognition and Technology Group at Vanderbilt).** Video adventure stories with embedded data that pose a complex maths problem. In early evaluation, 221 Jasper students did significantly better than comparison students on one-step, two-step and multi-step word problems by year end, and had better attitudes. Evidence comes largely from the developers; effects depended on the teaching model used in the classroom; the series is no longer maintained. — [EdWeek 1996](https://www.edweek.org/education/learning-with-jasper/1996/01); [EBSCO research starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/anchored-instruction); [ASCD](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/taking-word-problems-off-the-page)

### Inferences
- The narrative advantage in Mar et al. is for content that is itself narrative. The practical implication is to make the lesson's causal chain the plot's causal chain (the packet really must be split, routed and reassembled for the heroine's message to arrive), not to place expository paragraphs inside a story frame.
- "Thin story plus tight mechanic" beats "medium story with content bolted on" in the games data. For DreamWeaver the story is the product, so the risk to manage is story depth that is unrelated to the concept: every scene of a lesson story should either advance the concept or be short.
- Scaffolding matters more than story richness: hints, worked steps and explanatory feedback inside the story are the highest-yield additions (g about 0.4).
- Stylised/schematic illustration is not a learning handicap and may help; photorealism is not needed for lesson stories (correlational moderator evidence; note the picture-book literature in section 9 favours realistic depiction for toddlers' word learning, a different age and task).
- Listening works as well as reading for the narrative advantage, which supports AI narration.

### Gaps
- McQuiggan, Rowe, Lee and Lester (2008) "Story-based learning" outcome figures (full narrative versus minimal narrative versus slides) were not retrieved.
- Effect sizes for Adams et al. (2012) were not retrieved.
- No independent meta-analysis of anchored instruction found; transfer claims rest on developer studies.
- No strong evidence found on story-embedded teaching of technology concepts (networks, computers) to children specifically.

## 5. Seductive details, the coherence principle and multimedia principles for illustrated text

### Takeaway
Interesting but concept-irrelevant material reliably reduces learning of the target material, with a small-to-moderate average penalty, worse when the detail sits next to the relevant content or is constantly present. The cure is not "less story" but making the interesting bits be the relevant bits, signalling what matters, and keeping pictures and words about the same thing together.

### Cited Findings
- **Sundararajan and Adesope (2020, Educational Psychology Review 32(3), 707 to 734)**, 58 studies, more than 7,500 participants: learners given seductive details performed worse on learning outcomes than those without. The effect was larger on paper than in digital formats, more prominent in social studies and natural sciences, worse when the seductive detail was placed next to informative, relevant diagrams, and worse when it was static/constantly present (for example a static joke or image). Most studies used short sessions of 6 to 12 minutes and measured cognitive outcomes only. — [WSU news release](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [article record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- Moderators named in the underlying dissertation: nature of the intervention, number of media, format of the seductive detail, and number of sources used to develop materials. — [Sundararajan dissertation record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/SEDUCTIVE-DETAILS-A-META-REGRESSION-AND-EMPIRICAL/99900581709501842)
- **Mayer's coherence principle**: people learn better when extraneous words, pictures and sounds are excluded. Reported as supported in 18 of 19 (or 23 of 23, depending on handbook edition) experimental tests, median effect size d = 0.86. — [Mayer, research-based principles (UNH copy)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)
- **Signalling principle** (cues that highlight the essential material): supported in 26 of 28 tests, median d = 0.70 in one edition's table (0.86 from 16 of 16 in an older edition). — [Mayer (UNH copy)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- **Redundancy principle** (adding on-screen captions that duplicate narration over graphics): weak, 8 of 12 tests, effect size about 0.10. — [Mayer (UNH copy)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- The Cambridge Handbook chapter groups five extraneous-processing principles: coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial contiguity (words near the corresponding part of the picture) and temporal contiguity (narration at the same time as the corresponding picture). — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)
- Convergent picture-book evidence: pop-ups and lift-the-flap features tended to hinder word, letter and fact learning unless the manipulative highlighted the educational content itself (for example a letter's shape). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Convergent fantasy evidence: fantastical elements helped problem-solving transfer when they were relevant to the solution and distracted when incidental (Richert and Schlesinger 2016, as reviewed). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Caution on Mayer's medians: they come largely from his own laboratory's short lessons with college students on mechanical/scientific explanations; editions of the handbook report different counts and medians for the same principle. — [Mayer (UNH copy)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)

### Inferences
- A story is not automatically a seductive detail. The definition is "interesting but irrelevant to the learning goal". The rule for lesson packs: the most vivid, emotional, surprising moment in a scene should be the concept at work, not a side attraction next to it.
- The scene illustration on a concept beat should depict the concept (the message splitting into numbered pieces), not an unrelated spectacle; the worst placement for decorative interest is right beside the explanatory content.
- Use in-story signalling: a recurring visual motif or a character's catchphrase when a key idea appears; bold or repeat the key vocabulary term once at the moment it does work in the plot.
- Keep one key idea per scene, and keep the picture and the narrated sentence about that idea on the same screen at the same time (spatial and temporal contiguity).
- Narration plus identical full on-screen text is at worst a weak cost for adults (redundancy effect about 0.10); for children learning to read, read-along text has other benefits not covered by this literature. Offer it as a toggle.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes for Sundararajan and Adesope (2020) and Rey (2012) were not retrievable in this session (paywalled). Widely circulated figures (an overall negative effect around a third of a standard deviation; Rey reporting small-to-medium penalties for retention and transfer) should be verified at [doi 10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4) before quoting.
- Harp and Mayer's original (1997/1998) experiments were not read directly.
- Very little seductive-details research is on young children or on narrative fiction as the base material; most is on college students reading science explanations.
- Median effect sizes for spatial contiguity and personalisation (conversational style) were not retrieved.

## 6. Endogenous versus exogenous fantasy; intrinsic integration

### Takeaway
The best-controlled evidence (Habgood and Ainsworth 2011) supports putting the learning content inside the core mechanic rather than between rounds of fun: children learned more from the integrated version in the same time and, given free choice, played it about seven times longer. The evidence base is small (one game, small samples), and "integrated into the fantasy" is not the same as "more story".

### Cited Findings
- **Habgood and Ainsworth (2011, Journal of the Learning Sciences 20(2), 169 to 206).** Zombie Division teaches division to 7 to 11 year olds. Intrinsic version: division is the combat mechanic (choose the attack that divides the number on the enemy). Extrinsic version: the same game with division removed from combat and delivered as between-level quizzes. Study 1: 58 children played intrinsic, extrinsic or control versions for 2 hours with teacher support; children learned more from the intrinsic version under fixed time. Study 2 (free choice, 16 children): children spent 7 times longer on the intrinsic version. — [Habgood & Ainsworth 2011 (Sheffield Hallam repository)](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556); [Nottingham repository record](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577510/motivating-children-to-learn-effectively-exploring-the-value-of-intrinsic-integration-in-educational-games)
- The concept descends from Malone and Lepper's distinction between endogenous (intrinsic) fantasy, where skill and fantasy depend on each other, and exogenous fantasy, where the fantasy is a reward wrapper; Habgood's thesis argues the critical integration is with the core mechanic, not with the fantasy as such. — [Habgood PhD thesis (Nottingham)](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/10385/1/Habgood_2007_Final.pdf)
- Consistent with this, at the meta-analytic level more story depth and more contextualisation were not associated with more learning (see Clark et al. 2016 in section 4). — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- Fantastical story elements aid transfer when relevant to the solution and distract when incidental (Richert and Schlesinger 2016, as reviewed). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)

### Inferences
- In DreamWeaver the core mechanic is "pick one of the choices". Intrinsic integration therefore means: the choice that advances the plot is decided by the concept. The reader must use the idea (which gate lets through only the right kind of message?) to pick, and the story outcome is the feedback.
- The extrinsic anti-pattern is a quiz card between scenes, or a story that pauses for a narrator explanation and then resumes unchanged. The Zombie Division extrinsic condition was exactly "same game, quiz in between" and it lost on both learning and voluntary time.
- A lesson pack should specify, for each key idea, a story situation in which the idea has consequences, and for each misconception a tempting choice that fails for the reason the misconception is wrong.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes from Habgood and Ainsworth were not retrieved; the free-choice sample was only 16 children.
- A later attempt to test the intrinsic-integration hypothesis with a complex-numbers game was surfaced only as a snippet; its results are unknown ([eScholarship item](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8453p0gf/qt8453p0gf.pdf?t=sgiu9f)). Independent replication status is therefore unclear.
- Malone and Lepper (1987) was not read directly.

## 7. Learning-science techniques that could be built into story choices: strength of evidence, especially for children

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing have the strongest and broadest support, including in children and classrooms; self-explanation and refutation of misconceptions have moderate, fairly consistent support; productive failure works on average for older learners but reverses for 2nd to 5th graders, who do better with instruction first. Explanatory feedback and scaffolding beat bare right/wrong signals.

### Cited Findings
- **Overall ranking (Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest).** Ten techniques rated: high utility for practice testing and distributed practice ("benefit learners of different ages and abilities"); moderate utility for elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice; low utility for summarisation, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text and rereading. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html); [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- **Retrieval practice / testing effect.** Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research): practice tests are more beneficial than restudying and all other comparison conditions. — [Adesope et al. 2017 record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842)
- Rowland (2014, Psychological Bulletin): testing beats restudy; recall formats beat recognition formats and benefits are larger at retention intervals of a day or more than at short intervals (as summarised in later work). — [Rowland 2014 (PDF)](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Classroom evidence: Yang, Luo, Vadillo and Yu (2021) synthesised 222 independent studies with 48,478 students and found a reliable advantage of testing in real classrooms; classroom studies span elementary school to medical school. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf); [Frontiers in Education review of classroom testing](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00005/full)
- **Spacing / distributed practice.** A 2025 meta-analysis restricted to classroom studies found a moderate advantage for distributed over massed practice, d = 0.54 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.77), larger with longer retention intervals and older learners; an earlier broad synthesis (Donoghue and Hattie) reported d = 0.85 without separating lab from classroom. Classroom findings are less uniform than lab findings. — [2025 classroom meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12189222/)
- Children specifically: Vlach and Sandhofer (2012) taught food-chain concepts to young children on massed, clumped or spaced schedules; spacing produced better memory and better generalisation of the concept. — [Vlach & Sandhofer 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3399982)
- **Productive failure (Kapur).** Sinha and Kapur (2021, Review of Educational Research; 53 studies, 166 comparisons): problem-solving before instruction beats instruction before problem-solving for conceptual knowledge and transfer, g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51), rising to g = 0.37 to 0.58 with high fidelity to productive-failure design, and with a larger bias-adjusted estimate (g = 0.87). Crucially, "contrasting trends were ... observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored" instruction first. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4KnMgZq7)
- **Self-explanation.** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi and Winne (2018, Educational Psychology Review; 64 reports, 69 effect sizes): prompting learners to explain yields g = .55 overall; interrogative prompts (questions) g = 0.56 versus imperative prompts g = 0.40; versus no additional explanation g = 0.67. — [Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [Clearinghouse Unterricht summary table](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/KR_25_ENG_Table.pdf)
- In 5 and 6 year olds, explanation prompts during a storybook improved extraction of the underlying lesson over merely recalling events (Walker and Lombrozo 2017). — [Walker & Lombrozo 2017](https://cognition.princeton.edu/publications/explaining-moral-story)
- **Refutation texts for misconceptions.** Schroeder and Kucera (2022, Educational Psychology Review; 44 comparisons, n = 3,869): refutation text (state the misconception, say it is wrong, explain the correct idea) beats standard text, g = 0.41. Danielson and colleagues (2024, Educational Psychologist; 76 studies, 294 effect sizes) confirm a consistent, significant advantage in controlled experiments. — [Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z); [Danielson et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10629626)
- **Feedback and scaffolding in games.** In Clark et al. (2016), enhanced scaffolding g = 0.48 and answer display g = 0.40 versus success/fail/points only g = 0.26; teacher-provided scaffolding g = 0.58 and significantly better than bare success/fail feedback even after controls. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)
- **Curiosity before the answer** improves retention of the answer (adult lab evidence; see section 3). — [review, PMC6891259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmc6891259)

### Inferences
- Checks for understanding can be retrieval practice in disguise: a later scene in which a character asks the reader to recall or use the idea ("remind me, how did we get the message through last time?"). Free recall or having to choose based on remembered principle is better than re-reading an explanation. Recognition (pick from choices) is weaker than recall but still beneficial, and choices are DreamWeaver's native format.
- Spacing: re-use each key idea in at least two or three later scenes, and again in a later session or a sequel story, rather than concentrating it in one explanatory scene. "Previously on..." recaps that ask instead of tell are spaced retrieval.
- For children under about 11, do not rely on "let them fail first, explain later". Give a short, clear in-story demonstration (a worked example: a mentor character does it once), then let the reader apply it. For teens and adults, a "try to solve it before the reveal" beat is supported.
- Misconception handling maps directly onto branching: make the common misconception a selectable choice; when chosen, the story shows the consequence, a character names the mistaken idea explicitly, says it does not work, and gives the correct explanation (refutation structure), then offers the choice again in a fresh situation. Never punish with a dead end and no explanation: bare fail feedback is the weakest condition.
- Phrase understanding prompts as questions from a character ("Why do you think that worked?") rather than instructions ("Explain why"), consistent with the interrogative-prompt advantage.
- The lesson-pack schema implied by this evidence: key idea; in-plot situation where it has consequences; one worked demonstration; 1 or 2 misconceptions each with a tempting wrong choice and a refutation line; vocabulary with one in-context use; 2 or 3 spaced retrieval/application moments; one explain-why prompt; one transfer situation with different surface features.

### Gaps
- The exact pooled effect in Adesope et al. (2017) and child-specific subgroup estimates for testing were not confirmed in retrieved text.
- Feedback timing (immediate versus delayed) for children: no meta-analytic source was retrieved in this session; I cannot give a sourced recommendation beyond "explanatory beats bare right/wrong".
- Worked examples and Bjork's "desirable difficulties" framework were not separately sourced here; retrieval, spacing and interleaving above are its main components. Interleaving is rated only "moderate" by Dunlosky et al.
- Subgroup g for 2nd to 5th graders in Sinha and Kapur was not retrieved (direction only).
- Almost none of these techniques has been tested inside narrative fiction; transferring them to story choices is an inference.

## 8. Analogy and metaphor for technical concepts; anthropomorphism in science stories for children

### Takeaway
Analogies help when the learner maps the relational structure (what does what to what), and hurt when learners carry over surface features or over-extend the analogy; the standard safeguard is to make the mapping explicit and say where the analogy breaks down. For anthropomorphism the evidence is split: one study found human-like language about animals reduced factual learning and led children to attribute human traits to real animals, another found no interference.

### Cited Findings
- Structure-mapping (Gentner): an analogy is an alignment of two representations on the basis of their common relational structure, not shared surface attributes; learners benefit from guidance during the mapping process (Richland, Holyoak and Stigler 2004, as cited). — [eScholarship paper on analogy instruction](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6fq355bv/qt6fq355bv.pdf)
- Analogies are "double-edged swords" that can promote understanding and also lead to misconceptions; students may use an analogy mechanically without considering what it was meant to convey. — [Orgill & Bodner, chemistry education review](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/chemed/bodnergroup/PDF_2008/77%20Orgill%20CERP.pdf)
- Glynn's Teaching-With-Analogies model: introduce the target, recall the familiar analogue, identify and map the similar features, indicate "where the analogy breaks down", and draw conclusions; without the breakdown step students may fail to benefit. — [CSUN science teaching sourcebook](https://www.csun.edu/science/books/sourcebook/chapters/10-analogies/teaching-analogies.html)
- One review cited there found positive effects of analogies in 12 of 15 science-education studies; much less research addresses how to optimise them. — [Orgill & Bodner](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/chemed/bodnergroup/PDF_2008/77%20Orgill%20CERP.pdf)
- **Anthropomorphism harms (one study).** Ganea, Canfield, Simons-Ghafari and Chou (2014, Frontiers in Psychology, "Do cavies talk?"): 3 to 5 year olds saw picture books about novel animals with either factual or anthropomorphic language (and realistic or anthropomorphic pictures). Anthropomorphic language led children to attribute human characteristics to real animals and reduced younger children's learning of novel facts, even when pictures were realistic. — [Ganea et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989584)
- Brief exposure to an anthropomorphic bear storybook affected children's reasoning about bears' biology (Waxman et al. 2014, as reviewed). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- **Anthropomorphism does not harm (another study).** Geerdts, Van de Walle and LoBue (2016, Imagination, Cognition and Personality 36(1)): anthropomorphic language and pictures did not interfere with factual learning about real animals (camouflage) and did not produce unrealistic beliefs; the authors suggest anthropomorphism might be used as an educational tool. — [Geerdts et al. 2016 summary (NAAEE)](https://eepro.naaee.org/research/eeresearch/learning-about-real-animals-anthropomorphic-media)
- In games, medium anthropomorphism was associated with smaller learning effects (g = 0.04) than low/none (g = 0.37), but this was not significant after controls and high anthropomorphism (g = 0.55, 6 effects) did not fit a linear pattern. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/)

### Inferences
- For "how technology works" stories the plot is usually an extended analogy (packets as letters, a router as a postmaster). Design rules: (1) choose analogies whose relations match (who sends what to whom, what happens on failure), not just ones that look similar; (2) have a character make the mapping explicit at least once ("the address on the envelope is like..."); (3) include a deliberate "but unlike a real letter..." beat for each analogy; (4) in the lesson pack, list for each analogy what maps, what does not, and the misconception it is most likely to create.
- Personified technology (a chatty packet, a grumpy firewall) risks children attributing intention, feelings or understanding to machines. This is a live concern for an AI-narrated product in particular. Mitigation: keep the personified character's behaviour rule-governed and consistent with the real mechanism, and let a human character state the non-anthropomorphic version in plain language at least once.
- Use two different analogies or story contexts for the same idea where possible; comparison across cases is how structure-mapping theory says the relational core is abstracted (inference from the theory; not directly sourced here).

### Gaps
- Gentner's primary papers (structure mapping 1983; the relational shift in children) and Richland and Simms (2015) were not retrieved; the claim that young children favour surface over relational similarity is standard in this literature but unsourced here.
- No studies found on anthropomorphising technology/computers in children's stories and its effect on understanding of how machines work.
- No quantitative meta-analysis of analogy instruction for children retrieved.

## 9. Fantasy versus realistic contexts for children's learning and transfer (the "reader's dilemma")

### Takeaway
Mixed and domain-dependent. Young children are cautious about exporting information from stories, and several studies show better transfer of problem solutions, causal facts and prosocial behaviour from realistic stories; but other studies show equal or better learning from fantastical stories, especially for physics/biology content and vocabulary, and fantasy helps when the fantastical element is itself relevant to what is being taught. Transfer improves with age.

### Cited Findings
- The "reader's dilemma" (Gerrig and Prentice 1991): readers of fiction must decide which story information is true of the real world and should be exported, and which belongs only to the story. Hopkins and Weisberg (2017, Developmental Review, "The youngest readers' dilemma") review how children handle this. — [Hopkins & Weisberg 2021 (author manuscript)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10309037)
- Children can be "naive skeptics", tending to reject unrealistic information, so they may also dismiss accurate facts embedded in fantastical stories. In Walker et al. (2014), children transferred a fictional causal relation more often from realistic than fantastical stories; the tendency decreased with age as fantasy-reality discrimination improved. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- **Richert and colleagues.** Richert et al. (2009): 3.5 to 5.5 year olds were more likely to transfer solutions to novel real problems from stories with real characters than fantasy characters. Richert and Smith (2011, Child Development): the same pattern with full-length commercial picture books. Richert and Schlesinger (2016): fantastical elements helped transfer when relevant to the solution, distracted when incidental. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/); [Ganea et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989584)
- A British Psychological Society digest reports that children more prone to fantasy struggled more to apply lessons from fantasy stories. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/fantasy-prone-children-struggle-apply-lessons-fantasy-stories)
- **Counter-evidence.** Weisberg and Hopkins (2020, Infant and Child Development): preschoolers learned and exported information from fantastical stories about as well as from realistic ones, if not better. Hopkins and Weisberg (2021): children have "learned biology and physics information best from stories that violated real-world physical laws". — [Hopkins & Weisberg 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10309037)
- Physics: children improved on a balance task whether they read a realistic or a fantastical book (Ganea et al. 2017). Vocabulary: fantastical contexts supported more word knowledge in open-ended responses (Weisberg et al. 2015), though transfer was not tested. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Prosocial behaviour: human-character version increased sharing, anthropomorphic-animal version did not (Larsen et al. 2017; section 2). — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Review recommendations: help children separate real from pretend and discuss which story details are true; use generic language ("routers send messages along the fastest path") to signal that information applies beyond the story; talk about underlying structure; ask children to explain or to teach it to someone else. "The most supportive thing adults can do" is to converse with children during reading. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Pictorial realism matters most for the youngest: toddlers transferred words best from photographs and worst from cartoons. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)

### Inferences
- Fantasy settings are acceptable for lesson stories if (a) the fantastical element is the vehicle for the concept rather than decoration, (b) the story explicitly marks what is true in the real world, and (c) the idea is restated at least once in generic, real-world language.
- A short "back in our world" coda or a character who says "real computers do this too: ..." is a cheap, evidence-aligned export cue. It can be delivered by a character in the frame story rather than as a narrator lecture.
- For the youngest audience (under about 6) and for behavioural/prosocial lessons, lean realistic: human characters, familiar situations. For 7+ and for mechanism knowledge, fantasy is lower risk.
- Offering a parent/educator co-reading prompt ("ask your child: which parts of this could really happen?") matches the review's strongest recommendation.

### Gaps
- Most studies involve 3 to 6 year olds and single short picture books; evidence for ages 7 to 12 and for interactive or serial stories is thin.
- Full text of Hopkins and Weisberg (2017) review and Richert's 2022 "levels of fantasy" paper were not read.
- No studies found comparing fantasy and realistic framings for technology concepts.

## 10. Ethical considerations when stories are designed to persuade or teach, especially for children

### Takeaway
The same mechanisms that make stories effective (reduced counter-arguing, lower perceived persuasive intent, effects that persist for months, children's difficulty separating story-truth from real-truth) are what make them ethically sensitive. I found little dedicated empirical or normative research on the ethics of narrative persuasion for children; the points below are mostly implications drawn from the empirical findings and should be presented as such.

### Cited Findings
- Narrative persuasion works partly by reducing critical thoughts and counter-arguing and by lowering perception of persuasive intent. — [van Laer et al. 2014 record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/the-extended-transportation-imagery-model-a-meta-analysis-of-the-/); [Cambridge core reader on Moyer-Gusé & Nabi](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/07CABFC5ABA0D3A6A369FCDDB21F8F76/core-reader)
- Effects on attitudes, intentions and behaviour persist up to six months after a single exposure in adults. — [Oschatz & Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)
- Narrative persuasion is used in entertainment and public campaigns and also in "populist propaganda of politically motivated groups". — [Oschatz & Marker 2020](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110000/2026/OschatzMarker.pdf)
- Children can absorb false beliefs from stories: anthropomorphic language led 3 to 5 year olds to attribute human traits to real animals; brief storybook exposure changed biological reasoning about bears. — [Ganea et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989584); [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Young children's ability to separate fantasy from reality and decide what to export is still developing. — [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/)
- Children's literature scholarship argues that ideology is transmitted through hidden strategies in children's fantasy, and that hidden ideologies are no less persuasive for being hidden (Sutherland, as quoted in secondary sources). — [James Cook University thesis](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/31131/1/JCU_31131_Giardina_2011_thesis.pdf)
- An educational commentary argues the constructive response is to teach children to analyse persuasion rather than only shield them from it. — [ASCD](https://ascd.org/el/articles/response---channel-one-asking-the-wrong-questions)
- Claims of benefit should be modest: single-story persuasion effects are small, "fiction builds empathy" is weakly supported, and narrative games have sometimes taught less than plain instruction. — [Braddock & Dillard 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555); [Wimmer et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38602788/); [ATD summary of Adams et al. 2012](https://www.td.org/insights/why-games-dont-teach)

### Inferences
- Transparency at the frame level, subtlety at the story level: tell the parent/educator (and, age-appropriately, the child) on the story's cover or set-up screen what the story is meant to teach, while keeping the story itself free of sermonising. This preserves low in-story reactance without covert intent.
- Restrict designed persuasion to educator-authored, inspectable lesson packs; make the pack (key ideas, intended takeaways) visible to the adult. Avoid AI-improvised value messaging outside the pack.
- Accuracy duty: because children export story content (including errors and anthropomorphic framings), factual content in lesson stories needs a correctness check against the pack, and each analogy needs its "where it breaks down" line.
- Do not use transportation and identification for commercial ends inside the story (upsells voiced by a beloved character, cliff-hangers gated by payment): the mechanisms that lower counter-arguing make this manipulative, particularly for children.
- Build in agency: explain-why prompts and "what would you do?" choices keep the reader reasoning rather than only absorbing, which is both better pedagogy (section 7) and a partial ethical safeguard.
- Marketing claims should match evidence: "designed around retrieval practice and spaced re-use" is defensible; "proven to build empathy" or "kids learn better from stories" without qualification is not.

### Gaps
